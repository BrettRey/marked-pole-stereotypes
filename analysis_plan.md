## Hypotheses

- H1: P(attribute produced as a stereotype) rises with markedness (formal negation, by affix type; order markedness where covered; see Variables), controlling for valence and log frequency.
- H2: within valence, rarer attributes are more often stereotypic.
- **Open decision (raised 2026-10-07, Brett to settle before FIXED): what H1 estimates.** The hypothesis has trait rarity as a common cause of stereotypicality and of overt marking. Log word frequency is another rarity signal, and so is valence (UWA's positivity prevalence). Conditioning on both may absorb the shared path, so a pure common-cause account predicts a near-null conditional markedness effect, and a positive one would point to a further channel (the marking itself). Frequency may also be partly downstream of stereotyping, which makes it a poor control. Candidate structure: (a) markedness without the frequency control, as the common-cause test; (b) the change when log frequency is added, read as how much of (a) frequency accounts for; (c) positive words containing a negator (*flawless*, *blameless*), where the marked-pole and valence accounts predict differently. Caveat: *flawless* is privative *-less* on a noun, and its antonym *flawed* is also derived, so it isn't clearly the marked member of its pair.
- Strand A predictions (from the brief): fresh-sample PPV at chance (1/K) with no true differences; high valence agreement and low content agreement across perceivers with no true differences, content agreement rising with true differences.
- **Open decision (raised 2026-10-07 by a reviewing agent; Brett to settle before FIXED): the brief's last strand A step, mapping how large true differences must be for simulated content agreement to reach observed stereotype consensus.** The observed data don't measure what the simulation's perceivers do. UWA asked for stereotypes "you know that exist in society", and Ingendahl et al. for what "society attributes"; agreement in such answers mostly reflects shared cultural knowledge, which the consensus split leaves out. Matching the two would put all observed agreement down to true group differences. Adding a transmission parameter doesn't rescue the mapping, because one agreement statistic can't separate true differences from transmission. Recommended: drop the quantitative mapping and keep the qualitative result (sampling alone yields consensus on valence but not on content, so observed content consensus needs true differences, cultural transmission, or both). Reframe only if a dataset turns up that elicits perceivers' own beliefs.

## Proposed revisions (2026-10-07; written in at Brett's request, open until FIXED)

**Order of work.** (1) Strand A, with the group-size extension; (2) design simulation D1, built on strand A's engine; (3) the marking submodel on lexical data (L1, L2); (4) the joint model on stereotype responses (H2, H1). Steps 1–3 touch no outcome data, so they settle what the design can identify, and whether the hypothesis's "because" survives, before any stereotype responses are opened.

**The measurement problem.** H2, L1 and L2 concern how common a trait or state is among people (UWA's π), not how often its word is used. Word frequency measures how much a trait is talked about. That mixes prevalence with stakes (O'Connor 2019, *Games and Kinds*, fn. 10: each term covers an area "about equally important from a payoff point of view"), and in free response it also has a direct accessibility effect. So rarity is treated as a latent variable, not read off a single proxy, and no prevalence measure is chosen by fiat. Wanted, and still being looked for: perceived-prevalence or endorsement norms for trait adjectives, and a baseline production task (UWA's Corollary 3 contrast). A new rating study would need ethics review (Brett's decision). Prevalence ratings generated by an LLM would be stand-in data and are excluded.

**Strand B as one generative model (replaces the separate tests and the earlier "reported as not run" rule).** Items are candidate trait words *i*; groups *g*; participants *p*.

- *Latent per item:* prevalence ρ*ᵢ* (logit scale) and stakes s*ᵢ*. Valence v*ᵢ* is observed (Warriner mean, with its SD as measurement error).
- *Measurement submodels:* log word frequency depends on ρ, s and v; each prevalence indicator that exists depends on ρ, with a desirability bias term (self-endorsement inflates desirable traits); stakes indicators (Warriner arousal, valence extremity) depend on s.
- *Marking submodel (lexical; L1, L2):* formal negation, by affix type, depends on within-valence rarity, total valence and stakes, with terms for morphological process (participial *un-*, adjectival *un-*, *-less* on nouns, opaque Latinate *in-*). L1 and L2 ask whether marking follows rarity at fixed valence (L1 among positive words, L2 among negative words, where stakes also enter).
- *Production submodel (H2, H1):* whether participant *p* produces word *i* for group *g*, against the denominator set, depends on ρ (H2), on marking beyond rarity (H1), on word frequency as accessibility, and on v, with varying intercepts for group, participant and item.

Where the indicators are weak, the posteriors for these coefficients will be wide, and that is reported along with prior sensitivity. Nothing is dropped as "not run". D1 says in advance how wide they're likely to be. Fitting latent variables across submodels bears on the engine choice (brms or PyMC; still open).

**What the model can and can't identify (settled by D1's pilot, 2026-10-07).** Any prevalence measure partly lines up with valence, and the data can't tell whether that overlap is real (common traits tend to be desirable) or the measure's own bias (people say desirable traits fit them). So the latent is *within-valence* rarity, and each equation carries a *total* valence term. Identifiable: at the same valence, are rarer traits more often marked (L1, L2) or more often produced as stereotypes (H2)? Is marking associated with production beyond rarity (H1)? **Not identifiable without an extra assumption:** does valence matter once rarity is known? That is the "rarity rather than valence" half of the marked-pole claim, and close to UWA's own argument. The plan therefore claims no rarity-versus-valence contrast. Routes that would make it testable, for Brett to choose: (a) assume a size for the prevalence measure's valence bias and report a sensitivity analysis over a plausible range; (b) use two prevalence measures whose valence biases differ in a known direction (e.g. self-endorsement against judged prevalence in others).

**Which rarity: settled (Brett, 2026-10-09): both perceived and actual prevalence, as far as the data allow.** Approach, in Gelman's spirit (model the measurement rather than choose a construct; where a bias can't be estimated, show how conclusions depend on it):

1. *Target:* actual prevalence as a latent variable. Effects are also reported for perceived prevalence, which needs fewer assumptions.
2. *Indicators,* each with its own item-level standard errors, its own valence term, and an informative prior on a shared (between-rater) bias: judged prevalence in others (likely bias: availability, i.e. tracking word familiarity or marking, modelled as a dependence on word frequency); self-endorsement norms (likely bias: desirability); behavioural or act-frequency data where they exist.
3. *Identification by what exists.* With two or more indicators whose biases differ, actual prevalence is partly triangulated and both constructs are estimated. With one indicator, perceived prevalence is estimated directly, and actual-prevalence effects appear only as a sensitivity analysis over a stated range of the shared bias.
4. *Simulate first (D1b, before FIXED):* a fake-data check of the multi-indicator design, recovering actual-prevalence effects when the biases differ, and the error of the one-indicator estimate when a shared bias exists.

Background: D1 converges only when the indicator's noise is known, from item-level standard errors, which capture rater disagreement only; a misjudgment raters share is absorbed into the latent. So a single indicator yields perceived prevalence, and D1's results are a best case. This settlement also serves the availability guard (two indicators with different biases) and reopens the rarity-versus-valence contrast (route (b) above) when the indicators' valence biases differ in a known direction. What can be done depends on the prevalence-norm search (running 2026-10-09).

**Act type (raised 2026-10-09 by the side session's handover; L2 settled, H1 open).** In French (Google Books 1950–2019), whether a bad trait word defers to its antonym in comparatives (*moins honnête que* rather than *plus malhonnête que*) follows act type: failing to do an expected act against doing acts, net of word frequency, logit 1.06 [0.51, 1.56] over 128 concepts. With act type in the model, formal negation has no detectable effect among traits (−0.13 [−0.67, 0.54], 21 prefixed traits), though across all adjectives negated words do defer more (`Project-Management/notes/2026-10-09-marked-pole-brief-for-main-session.md`; numbers checked against `2026-10-08-marked-pole-french-v2/results.md` and `results_1b.md`). Limits: model-coded act type (eight families, no human check), French, comparatives rather than stereotypes. It bears on two things here. (1) H1 measures markedness by formal negation; if the marked pole means the dependent pole, act type may be the better trait-level variable. (2) L2's illustrations confound stakes with act type (*cruel*, *violent* coded 'doing'; *désordonné* 'not doing'). **Settled for L2 (Brett, 2026-10-09): act type is adopted in L2** (see L2 below). Still open, for H1: keep formal negation and say the hypothesis concerns overt coding; add act type (commission against omission) as an observed item-level covariate in the marking and production submodels; or add the comparative measure itself as a functional markedness variable where English counts exist. Act type is close to the "feature absence" sense kept separate in Variables; the French result supports keeping it distinct from formal negation.

**Proposed (2026-10-09): word-level controls for H1.** D1's scenario 2 adds an unmeasured word property, learned or Latinate register, that lowers frequency, raises marking and raises production, with H1's true value set to 0. In pilot 2 (one fit per cell, v2.2), βm came out about +.20 with the 90% interval excluding 0 at the large size under both prevalence measures, and about +.23 with intervals touching 0 at the small size, while βz stayed near 0 (`results/d1/pilot2_fits.csv`). The spec anticipated this: a confounder of marking and production biases H1 however well prevalence is measured. Only measuring the confounder helps. So H1's model should include word-level controls: etymological origin or register (Latinate against native), and act type (see the open decision above). Sources for etymology and register are still to choose and verify. The D1 grid, when it runs, will put a rate on the false positives (20 reps per cell).

**H1 and the frequency control.** Gelman & Hill (2007, §9.7, p. 188): "it is generally not a good idea to control for variables measured after the treatment." If word frequency is partly a consequence of trait rarity, it's an intermediate outcome, and §9.8 cautions against regressing on intermediate outcomes to estimate mediation. So frequency enters the joint model as a modelled path, not as a covariate regressed out. This supersedes the "(b) the change when log frequency is added" reading in the open decision above.

**D1: design simulation (no outcome data).** Fake-data simulation in the sense of the *Bayesian Workflow* paper (Gelman et al. 2020, §4.1, p. 16). Two checks:

1. *Parameter recovery:* simulate from the joint model with known coefficients, fit it, and compare.
2. *Mechanism check:* simulate stereotype production from strand A's process (perceivers selecting high-PPV attributes from finite samples), add a word-frequency channel with accessibility and stakes effects, and fit the joint model. Does it recover the rarity signature from a process it doesn't share?

Conditions: word frequency as the only rarity indicator; word frequency plus one prevalence indicator at several reliabilities; plausible effect sizes; item, group and participant counts matching the real datasets once verified. Reported per coefficient: interval coverage, bias, interval width, and sign-error rate and exaggeration ratio (Gelman & Carlin 2014). The results decide whether a prevalence indicator is essential for each coefficient. Brett makes that call with D1's output in hand, before the plan is FIXED. Simulated data is used only to evaluate the design. It's never analysed as evidence and never stands in for a dataset that should come from a source.

**Strand A, extension A5: unequal group sizes.** UWA say their account "does not require assumptions about minority status or group size" (Cognitive Principle section), but their simulation fixes 10 groups of 100. Vary the target group's share at fixed total N and report UWA's three outcomes by share. Comparison point, not a shared mechanism: the cultural Red King effect (O'Connor 2019, *Origins of Unfairness*, ch. 6), where minority disadvantage comes from learning speed in strategic games. Optional A6: three-valued attributes (positive, neither, negative), per Variables.

**L1: rare-positive domains (lexical; no outcome data).** If marking follows rarity, then in an opposition where the positive pole is the rare one, the positive member should carry the negator. UWA name ability as a domain where "positive information may be rare". Candidate words (assembled from intuition; check against a lexicon before use): *unbeaten*, *undefeated*, *unmatched*, *unrivalled*, *faultless*, *infallible*, *invincible*. Restrict the test to oppositions where exactly one member contains a negator; privative pairs such as *harmless/harmful* and *flawless/flawed* have both members derived. Control the morphological process: participial *un-* attaches more freely than adjectival *un-*, *-less* attaches to nouns, and Latinate *in-* is opaque in English. Against the hypothesis: negated positive members are no more common where the positive pole is rarer, measured by prevalence and not by intuition.

**L2: stakes within negative words (lexical; no outcome data; a conjecture).** If lexicalization follows payoff importance and short forms follow frequency of use (form-frequency correspondence; Haspelmath & Karjus 2017, `literature/10.1515_ling-2017-0026.md`, to read), then among negative person-descriptive adjectives, formal negation should cluster among low-stakes words (*impolite*, *untidy*, *unpunctual*) and simple forms among high-stakes ones (*cruel*, *violent*). The examples are illustrations, not data. Stakes proxy: Warriner arousal or valence extremity, both crude. With prevalence in the same model, L2 asks whether marking follows trait rarity or how much a trait is talked about. If marking follows stakes and not prevalence, the hypothesis's single-rarity claim fails. **Act type (adopted for L2, Brett, 2026-10-09):** each word is coded for act type, naming acts done (commission) or an expected act not done (omission), and act type enters the L2 model as an item-level covariate. In the side session's French coding, the L2 illustrations split along it (*cruel* and *violent* coded 'doing', *désordonné* 'not doing'; per its brief), so without it a stakes effect could be an act-type effect. The coding source (the side session's multi-family instrument, `Project-Management/notes/2026-10-08-marked-pole-french-v2/prompts/code.txt`, or human coders) is fixed with the plan.

**H1: exploratory.** Formal negation of evaluative adjectives is nearly fixed by valence (*un-* attaches to positive bases and yields negatives), so after a valence control the estimate rests on a small, mixed off-diagonal set. The one item-level test of frequency against markedness (Ingram et al. 2016) found nothing beyond valence. H1 is estimated in the joint model (the marking-beyond-rarity coefficient) and treated as exploratory.

## Variables

**Senses of "negative" (settled 2026-10-07; see `DECISIONS.md`).** Four senses stay separate, each with its own variable or none:

- **Valence**: the attribute is evaluated as bad. Warriner rating. In this plan, "negative attribute" and "positive attribute" mean valence and nothing else.
- **Formal negation**: the word contains a negator. Coded by type, because the types differ in meaning: contrary prefixes (*un-*, *in-/im-/il-/ir-*, *dis-*), *non-*, and privative *-less*. Record the base's valence too (*flawless* negates a negative base, *flaw*, and yields a positive word). Hand-check detections (*uniform*, *discuss*).
- **Order markedness**: Ingram, Hand & Maciejewski's (2016) markedness probability, where their 154 pairs cover the word. A behavioural measure, not a morphological one.
- **Scalar polarity**: the less-of pole of a dimension (*short*, *few*). Valence-neutral; not used as a markedness measure for H1.

**Feature absence** (UWA's presence/absence coding, πneg = 1 − πpos) is a modelling choice in strand A, not a property of words. Affixal negation isn't treated as absence: *un-* on an evaluative adjective usually gives a contrary (someone can be neither friendly nor unfriendly). If strand A is extended to carry marking, an attribute needs three values (positive, neither, negative), closer to UWA's range principle than to their binary coding.

Avoid "negative pole" and bare "marked" in the plan and report; name the sense.
