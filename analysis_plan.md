## Hypotheses

- H1 (exploratory, predictive; settled 2026-10-09, see "H1 details"): does formal negation improve prediction of which words are produced as stereotypes, beyond within-valence rarity, valence, word frequency and measured word properties (register, act type)? Not a test of the common-cause account; L1 and L2 test the rarity-to-marking link directly.
- H2: within valence, rarer attributes are more often stereotypic.
- **What H1 estimates (raised 2026-10-07; settled 2026-10-09 by "H1 details: settled", which supersedes the candidate structure below).** The hypothesis has trait rarity as a common cause of stereotypicality and of overt marking. Log word frequency is another rarity signal, and so is valence (UWA's positivity prevalence). Conditioning on both may absorb the shared path, so a pure common-cause account predicts a near-null conditional markedness effect, and a positive one would point to a further channel (the marking itself). Frequency may also be partly downstream of stereotyping, which makes it a poor control. Candidate structure: (a) markedness without the frequency control, as the common-cause test; (b) the change when log frequency is added, read as how much of (a) frequency accounts for; (c) positive words containing a negator (*flawless*, *blameless*), where the marked-pole and valence accounts predict differently. Caveat: *flawless* is privative *-less* on a noun, and its antonym *flawed* is also derived, so it isn't clearly the marked member of its pair.
- Strand A predictions (from the brief): fresh-sample PPV at chance (1/K) with no true differences; high valence agreement and low content agreement across perceivers with no true differences, content agreement rising with true differences. The low-content-agreement prediction holds for independent samples, not for perceivers sampling heavily overlapping members of one small society (see the open decision below).
- **Settled (Brett, 2026-10-09: "drop it, keep forward map"): the brief's last strand A step, mapping how large true differences must be for simulated content agreement to reach observed stereotype consensus.** The observed data don't measure what the simulation's perceivers do. UWA asked for stereotypes "you know that exist in society", and Ingendahl et al. for what "society attributes"; agreement in such answers mostly reflects shared cultural knowledge, which the consensus split leaves out. Matching the two would put all observed agreement down to true group differences. Adding a transmission parameter doesn't rescue the mapping, because one agreement statistic can't separate true differences from transmission. Adopted (revised 2026-10-09 after a Gelman pass and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-consensus-2026-10-09/`; settled by Brett the same day, since it changes a deliverable of the brief): no inverse calibration from observed consensus to true differences. The statistics aren't commensurable either (top-5 Jaccard over 100 simulated attributes against open-vocabulary responses, one per participant per group in UWA Study 2 and six in Nicolas et al.), and a comparison would need a matched observation model (vocabulary, response budget, selection rule, lexical accessibility) and constrained assumptions about exposure and sample overlap. Kept: the forward map, read conditionally, which gives for each sampling model how large true differences must be for content agreement to reach a given level, reported with the prevalence shifts and recovery, since consensus isn't accuracy (at n = 100 per group, δ = 1 gives independent-sample Jaccard .938 when the rare attributes differ and .106 when the common ones do; `results/strandA/SUMMARY.md`, section 5). The qualitative result needs the sampling structure stated: independent sampling yields valence consensus without content consensus, but sampling from a shared small society needn't (Jaccard .995 with no true differences at M = n = 100), so "sampling alone can't produce content consensus" is false as a general claim. Within-dataset contrasts (in-group against out-group perceivers, exposure, Ingendahl et al.'s countries) could test predicted variation with partial pooling and held-out prediction, but wouldn't identify transmission, since exposure, ecology and cultural knowledge covary.

**H2 details: settled (Brett, 2026-10-09, after an independent review by Codex, `reviews/codex-h2-2026-10-09/`).**

- *Estimand.* The primary quantity is a model-based marginal association: the posterior-predictive change in production for a 1 SD difference in within-valence rarity, averaging over word frequency **and marking** under the joint model. It is labelled an association, not a causal total effect, because frequency's causal position is uncertain and modelling it jointly doesn't establish mediation. Conditional coefficients (holding frequency and marking) are reported second. Orientation: z measures prevalence (higher = more common), so H2 predicts negative values.
- *Datasets.* UWA Study 1 for calibration only (response normalization; it informs neither the word list nor model selection). UWA Study 2 and Nicolas et al. are separate confirmatory tests with dataset-specific effects, not one pooled estimate. Ingendahl et al.: the Irish (English) subsample only. Participant dependence is modelled in both confirmatory datasets (UWA participants also answer several groups).
- *The word list (denominator).* Anderson's 555 plus person-descriptive adjectives from the Warriner norms. Eligibility rules (senses, participles, ambiguous adjectives) are frozen and coded independently of which words have prevalence norms, so that norm coverage can't select on familiarity or rarity. Pre-specified sensitivity analyses: Anderson only; the expanded list; norm-covered words only. Words formally negated with no extant root (see Variables) are kept, in their own category.
- *Response normalization.* Rules frozen after Study 1. Ambiguous responses are adjudicated blind to rarity and to the hypotheses. Syntactic negation, intensifiers and compounds are preserved, not mapped to affixal forms (*not friendly* is not *unfriendly*; cf. Beukeboom et al. 2010). Exclusions are reported by dataset, group and valence. Responses outside the eligible vocabulary are an explicit outcome category.
- *What counts against H2.* Report the interval, P(effect < 0) and P(effect < −.10). Meaningful support: the 90% interval lies wholly below −.10. Evidence against an effect that large: it lies wholly above −.10. Otherwise inconclusive. The .10 (per SD of within-valence rarity, log scale) must be justified substantively and translated into predicted response probabilities before the plan is FIXED.
- *Likelihood.* Production is modelled as a categorical choice among eligible words plus an outside option, matching fixed response budgets and competition among words, not as independent negative-binomial cells. The model includes group-specific content (group-by-item variation) and partially pooled group slopes; participant variation enters through slopes (a participant intercept common to all words cancels in a choice model). Posterior predictive checks: concentration, zeros, between-group overlap.
- *Design check before FIXED (D1c).* D1's production submodel is re-simulated with the categorical likelihood, with null and threshold-sized effects, indicator bias and prior sensitivity, and with the word-list size, coverage and Nicolas et al.'s clustering updated once the word list is frozen (D1 used placeholders: 400 items, 40% coverage, independent responses). D1's measurement-side conclusions are expected to carry over but are rechecked.

## Proposed revisions (2026-10-07; written in at Brett's request, open until FIXED)

**Order of work.** (1) Strand A, with the group-size extension; (2) design simulation D1, built on strand A's engine; (3) the marking submodel on lexical data (L1, L2); (4) the joint model on stereotype responses (H2, H1). Steps 1–3 touch no outcome data, so they settle what the design can identify, and whether the hypothesis's "because" survives, before any stereotype responses are opened.

**The measurement problem.** H2, L1 and L2 concern how common a trait or state is among people (UWA's π), not how often its word is used. Word frequency measures how much a trait is talked about. That mixes prevalence with stakes (O'Connor 2019, *Games and Kinds*, fn. 10: each term covers an area "about equally important from a payoff point of view"), and in free response it also has a direct accessibility effect. So rarity is treated as a latent variable, not read off a single proxy, and no prevalence measure is chosen by fiat. Wanted, and still being looked for: perceived-prevalence or endorsement norms for trait adjectives, and a baseline production task (UWA's Corollary 3 contrast). *Revised 2026-10-09 (Brett):* no research funding, so no paid participant study; and what the measurement model needs is a **judge** of commonness (an instrument, human or model, validated for this use against human data), not a participant sample. Model-judged commonness is therefore admissible as an instrument whose measurements are labelled as such and calibrated against the human panel in Ziano et al. (149 traits, about 80 raters each); it is never presented as human norms (that would be stand-in data). The earlier blanket exclusion of LLM ratings conflated an instrument with fabricated data.

**Strand B as one generative model (replaces the separate tests and the earlier "reported as not run" rule).** Items are candidate trait words *i*; groups *g*; participants *p*.

- *Latent per item:* prevalence ρ*ᵢ* (logit scale) and stakes s*ᵢ*. Valence v*ᵢ* is observed (Warriner mean, with the standard error of the mean, SD/√n, as measurement error).
- *Measurement submodels:* word frequency has two layers (revised 2026-10-09 after Dunning 1993 and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-sparsity-2026-10-09/`): a latent log usage rate per word, depending on ρ, s and v with its own between-word residual, and the corpus count given that rate and the corpus size (Poisson: with one count per word, a negative-binomial dispersion and the between-word residual aren't separately identified, so the residual carries the overdispersion, and a dispersion term is added only where the source's document counts can fix it), so the count noise that dominates for rare words is kept apart from substantive variation; the latent rate, not the observed frequency, enters production as accessibility, and D1c rechecks the design with it; each prevalence indicator that exists depends on ρ, with a desirability bias term (self-endorsement inflates desirable traits); stakes indicators (Warriner arousal, valence extremity) depend on s. Valence extremity can't be separated from a flexible valence term, nor from a linear one within one valence, so where valence enters flexibly (see Multiverse) stakes rest on arousal.
- *Marking submodel (lexical, word level):* formal negation, by affix type, depends on within-valence rarity, total valence and stakes, with terms for morphological process (participial *un-*, adjectival *un-*, *-less* on nouns, opaque Latinate *in-*). It asks whether, at the same valence, rarer words are more often negated, among positive words and among negative words (L2, where stakes and act type also enter). Which pole of an opposition carries the negator is a pair-level question, L1's, modelled separately (see L1).
- *Production submodel (H2, H1):* whether participant *p* produces word *i* for group *g*, against the denominator set, depends on ρ (H2), on marking beyond rarity (H1), on word frequency as accessibility, and on v, with varying intercepts for group, participant and item.

Where the indicators are weak, the posteriors for these coefficients will be wide, and that is reported along with prior sensitivity. Nothing is dropped as "not run". D1 says in advance how wide they're likely to be. Fitting latent variables across submodels bears on the engine choice (brms or PyMC; still open).

**What the model can and can't identify (settled by D1's pilot, 2026-10-07).** Any prevalence measure partly lines up with valence, and the data can't tell whether that overlap is real (common traits tend to be desirable) or the measure's own bias (people say desirable traits fit them). So the latent is *within-valence* rarity, and each equation carries a *total* valence term. Identifiable: at the same valence, are rarer traits more often marked (L1, L2) or more often produced as stereotypes (H2)? Is marking associated with production beyond rarity (H1)? **Not identifiable without an extra assumption:** does valence matter once rarity is known? That is the "rarity rather than valence" half of the marked-pole claim, and close to UWA's own argument. The plan therefore claims no rarity-versus-valence contrast. **Settled (Brett, 2026-10-09):** route (b), two prevalence measures whose valence biases differ in a known direction (self-endorsement inflates desirable traits; judged prevalence in others should do so less), where such measures exist; otherwise route (a), one measure with a sensitivity analysis over a stated range of its valence bias. The contrast is claimed only as strongly as the route supports; under (a), as a conditional ("if the measure's valence bias lies between X and Y, valence does or doesn't matter once rarity is known").

**What associations can and can't show (2026-10-09, after Kilgarriff 2005 and the Codex review above).** Language isn't random, so with enough data any association among rarity, marking and production will differ from zero, and a demonstrably non-random relation "does not support the inference that it is not arbitrary" (Kilgarriff 2005, p. 263). The inference for each effect is therefore its posterior on an interpretable scale, with the curve of posterior probabilities of exceeding a range of magnitudes in the predicted direction (BDA3 §4.5: "the question 'Does θ equal 0?' can generally be rephrased more usefully as 'What is the posterior distribution for θ?'"). The three-way labels (support, evidence against an effect that large, inconclusive) summarize it against reporting benchmarks, one per estimand, which Brett sets by judgement from concrete probability examples before FIXED; they're evaluative, not implied by the hypothesis, and kept separate from the plausible range used in design analysis (Gelman & Carlin 2014 separate "a realistic hypothesized effect size" from "a minimal effect size deemed to be substantively important"; revised 2026-10-09 after a Codex review, `reviews/codex-thresholds-2026-10-09/`). An estimand without a benchmark that has a principled interpretation is reported by its curves alone. For an equal-tailed 90% interval, lying wholly beyond a benchmark means a posterior probability above .95 of exceeding it. A large association still says nothing by itself about mechanism, since confounding and instrument bias can produce one. Measured controls and the D1 and D2 confound scenarios address only the alternatives they specify. L1, L2 and H2 share the word list and the commonness instrument, so agreement among them isn't independent corroboration.

**Which rarity: settled (Brett, 2026-10-09): both perceived and actual prevalence, as far as the data allow.** Approach, in Gelman's spirit (model the measurement rather than choose a construct; where a bias can't be estimated, show how conclusions depend on it):

1. *Target:* actual prevalence as a latent variable. Effects are also reported for perceived prevalence, which needs fewer assumptions.
2. *Indicators,* each with its own item-level standard errors, its own valence term, and an informative prior on a shared (between-rater) bias: judged prevalence in others (likely bias: availability, i.e. tracking word familiarity or marking, modelled as a dependence on word frequency); self-endorsement norms (likely bias: desirability); behavioural or act-frequency data where they exist.
3. *Identification by what exists.* With two or more indicators whose biases differ, the plausible range for actual prevalence narrows, but actual-prevalence effects stay sensitivity-based: differing biases don't identify actual prevalence without anchors or restrictions on shared bias, and priors supply assumptions, not identification (Codex review, 2026-10-09). With one indicator, perceived prevalence is estimated directly, and actual-prevalence effects appear only as a sensitivity analysis over a stated range of the shared bias.
4. *Simulate first (D1b, before FIXED):* a fake-data check of the multi-indicator design. Recovery is possible only under explicit identifying restrictions, which D1b must state algebraically (a shared bias, e.g. from familiarity, can be absorbed into the latent without changing the data); it reports conclusions across pre-specified bias, transport and feedback assumptions rather than a single recovery (corrected 2026-10-09 after the Codex review of the prevalence indicators, `reviews/codex-prevalence-2026-10-09/`). Known item-level standard errors constrain sampling error, not unknown systematic measurement error.

Background: D1 converges only when the indicator's noise is known, from item-level standard errors, which capture rater disagreement only; a misjudgment raters share is absorbed into the latent. So a single indicator yields perceived prevalence, and D1's results are a best case. This settlement also serves the availability guard (two indicators with different biases) and reopens the rarity-versus-valence contrast (route (b) above) when the indicators' valence biases differ in a known direction. What can be done depends on the prevalence-norm search (running 2026-10-09).

**Act type (raised 2026-10-09 by the side session's handover; L2 and H1 settled, see "H1 details").** In French (Google Books 1950–2019), whether a bad trait word defers to its antonym in comparatives (*moins honnête que* rather than *plus malhonnête que*) follows act type: failing to do an expected act against doing acts, net of word frequency, logit 1.06 [0.51, 1.56] over 128 concepts. With act type in the model, formal negation has no detectable effect among traits (−0.13 [−0.67, 0.54], 21 prefixed traits), though across all adjectives negated words do defer more (`Project-Management/notes/2026-10-09-marked-pole-brief-for-main-session.md`; numbers checked against `2026-10-08-marked-pole-french-v2/results.md` and `results_1b.md`). Limits: model-coded act type (eight families, no human check), French, comparatives rather than stereotypes. It bears on two things here. (1) H1 measures markedness by formal negation; if the marked pole means the dependent pole, act type may be the better trait-level variable. (2) L2's illustrations confound stakes with act type (*cruel*, *violent* coded 'doing'; *désordonné* 'not doing'). **Settled for L2 (Brett, 2026-10-09): act type is adopted in L2** (see L2 below). Settled for H1 the same day (see "H1 details"): formal negation stays the marking variable, and act type enters through a calibrated measurement model within a pre-specified set of specifications; the comparative measure isn't part of the settled specification. Act type is close to the "feature absence" sense kept separate in Variables; the French result supports keeping it distinct from formal negation.

**H1 details: settled (Brett, 2026-10-09, after a Gelman pass and an independent Codex review, `reviews/codex-h1-2026-10-09/`).**

- *What H1 asks.* An exploratory, predictive question: does formal negation improve held-out prediction of word choice beyond within-valence rarity, valence, word frequency and measured word properties? Prediction is evaluated by holding out **words or morphological families**, not participants (participant holdouts leave the word-level problem untouched). The conditional association is also reported as a posterior-predictive comparison averaged over a common-support population of words and groups, with the competing vocabulary fixed and the treatment of frequency (held fixed or integrated over) stated. Predictive improvement remains compatible with confounding, so H1 isn't read as evidence for or against the common-cause account.
- *Why not a test.* D1 scenario 2: an unmeasured register factor produced a marking coefficient of about +.20, with 90% intervals excluding 0 at the large size, where the truth was 0, under both prevalence measures (`results/d1/pilot2_fits.csv`). Only measuring the confounder helps, and adjustment can't rule out unmeasured ones.
- *Causal structures.* Alternative causal diagrams for marking, act type, register, frequency and production are written down before FIXED. Act type may partly reflect meaning created by negation, and register reflects lexical history and use, so neither is assumed to precede marking without argument.
- *Negation and act type.* Separate coefficients for contrary prefixes (*un-*, *in-/im-/il-/ir-*, *dis-*), *non-* and privative *-less*; affixes are pooled only within the contrary-prefix class. A sceptical prior on a pre-specified negation × act-type interaction, with a recovery check under sparse overlap. Act type enters through a measurement model (model-coded; see below) allowing correlated errors across coder families and genuinely ambiguous items, not as a thresholded code or a raw vote share.
- *No human calibration of act type (Brett, 2026-10-09: "It will have to be model judgments all the way").* Act type is model-coded: several model families code each concept from its morphology-free description, and a measurement model pools them with correlated errors across families and genuinely ambiguous items. Family agreement is a consistency check, not validation; without restrictions on shared error, the pooled latent is model-coded act type, not true act type. Sensitivity analyses vary shared misclassification, including misclassification that depends on negation, beside the adjusted and unadjusted causal alternatives.
- *Coding source (revised 2026-10-09, see L2).* Act type is coded by model families from the morphology-free descriptions written for L1, for every use (H1 and L2), since coding from the word invites "prefixed" to stand in for "omission". Word-coded act type, with instructions that guard against that shortcut, is a secondary specification showing how far lexical cueing moves the codes.
- *Register.* Three distinct measures, not one: etymological origin (Latinate against native), present-day register (learnedness or formality), and familiarity (for example Condon, Coughlin & Weston 2022, CC0). Sources still to choose and verify. D1c simulates how much bias survives across control reliabilities, correlations with valence and act type, and differential coding errors.
- *Reporting.* A pre-specified set of specifications (no word-level controls; register; act type; both), which isn't nested, each reported as standardized predictive contrasts on the same target population, with overlap diagnostics and prior sensitivity. No "interpret only if it survives" rule; coefficient movement isn't read as confounding removed. D1 shows bias is possible; it doesn't give false-positive rates.
- *Morphological bias.* A morphology-dependent bias in word-based commonness falls exactly on the negated words, so H1's marking coefficient is stated conditional on bounds on that bias, with description-based commonness as a separate indicator (see L2).
- *Overlap check.* Unprefixed negative words are informative contrasts too; check the overlap of negation and valence on the frozen word list before relying on the claim that the estimate rests on a small off-diagonal set.

**Multiverse (settled, Brett, 2026-10-09: "Gelman would usually say use them all. We take a multiverse approach"; design revised the same day after a Codex review, `reviews/codex-multiverse-2026-10-09/`).** Where several analytic options are defensible, all are run as a pre-specified multiverse (Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman & Vanpaemel 2016) and reported across, instead of one being chosen. Pre-specification constrains the fitting choices; the rules for ordering and emphasis in reporting are fixed in advance too, since otherwise emphasis after the fact would be its own forking path.

*What varies, and how it is reported, in three kinds:*

- **Alternative models of one estimand** (the multiverse proper): prevalence indicators (Ziano et al.'s commonness alone; with Ziano self- and other-ratings as separate auxiliary constructs; with the ESCS self-ratings as auxiliary endorsement data, never as a prevalence indicator; with the new ratings, adjective, behavioural or both, if collected); availability (a grid of shared familiarity bias and of direct morphological bias in the prevalence ratings; familiarity from Condon, Coughlin & Weston 2022); valence bias of the prevalence measure (a grid, for the rarity-versus-valence contrast); valence adjustment (linear and flexible); measurement-invariance and transport restrictions (with and without); feedback from outcomes to inferred rarity (joint, and restricted, "cut"); H1 controls (none, register, act type, both); response normalization (strict and lenient, both frozen after UWA Study 1); priors (reference, and substantively calibrated identifying priors, not merely widened ones).
- **Different estimands, reported side by side, never tallied together:** frequency held fixed and frequency integrated over, computed from the same posterior where possible.
- **Different target populations, reported as separate strata:** word sets (Ziano's 149; ESCS-covered; all eligible; Anderson only; the expanded list) and datasets (UWA Study 2, Nicolas et al., Ingendahl et al.'s Irish subsample). Each word set changes the population and the competing vocabulary, so it isn't a robustness check of another.

The categorical choice likelihood is fixed, not varied: independent negative-binomial counts contradict fixed response budgets and competition among words (see "H2 details"), and no sampling rationale supports them. Only admissible combinations are run; unavailable indicators and unsupported scale bridges aren't alternatives. For the lexical models (L1, L2), which are cheap, the relevant dimensions are crossed in full. For the joint production model, a fixed fractional-factorial or space-filling design covering the coupled assumptions (indicators × word sets × availability; availability × valence bias; H1 controls × measurement reliability and feedback) and the plausible bias extremes is chosen by simulation (D1b, D1c) before any stereotype outcomes are opened. Convergence criteria, retry rules, and the display of failed fits are registered; robustness is claimed only over the evaluated design.

*Model expansion where uncertainty is measured; separate fits where it isn't.* Measured uncertainties go inside one expanded model: respondent-level measurement with rater effects, instrument-specific loadings, uncertain act-type codes, partially pooled affix effects within the contrary-prefix class, and regularized nonlinearities. Assumptions the data can't adjudicate (shared bias restrictions, feedback policies) stay as separate sensitivity fits; averaging over them would hide the identifying assumptions inside prior weights.

*Reporting.* Every specification's estimate and interval, in a fixed pre-registered order, stratified by dataset, estimand and word set, on a common contrast scale (the reference scaling of "1 SD of rarity" is frozen across word sets where comparable, not re-standardized within each subset). The H2 evidence rule is applied to each admissible specification separately. Overall labels, fixed in advance: *uniform meaningful support* if every specification qualifies; *uniform evidence against an effect that large* if every one qualifies against; *uniformly inconclusive* if every specification is inconclusive; otherwise *conflicting*, with the classes shown. A change of label isn't itself an estimate (Gelman & Stern 2006), so estimates are compared directly: differences between datasets are estimated jointly where defensible, and differences between identifying assumptions stay sensitivity contrasts. The share of specifications meeting a criterion is descriptive only, not a probability (duplicating grid points would change it). Direction is reported separately from magnitude. Datasets aren't pooled into any tally.

**Commonness judges (replacing the paid rating study, which isn't funded; design settled 2026-10-09 after a Gelman pass and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-judges-2026-10-09/`; engine amended by Brett the same day).**

- *The human anchor.* Ziano et al.'s raw commonness ratings (149 traits, about 80 raters each), modelled at the respondent level, ordinal, with rater effects and posterior predictive checks of category use and item disagreement. The OSF data structure and instructions are verified first. The anchor measures this panel's perceived commonness, not actual prevalence; Ziano's wording also mixes "how many people have it" with "how often it's displayed".
- *Judges.* Several model families, local first, versions and prompts frozen (population, timeframe, trait interpretation, scale). Logprob-based expected ratings and chat ratings are distinct instruments; excluded probability mass and tokenization are checked; repeated chats measure stochastic variation, not independent evidence. Correlated errors across families and prompt effects are modelled, since several families can share one systematic error. A small pilot comes before the full vocabulary.
- *Calibration and transport.* Judges are linked to the human latent on the 149 with partially pooled discrepancies, regularized nonlinearities, correlated instrument errors and a systematic-error floor. Carrying calibration to the other words isn't automatic, since the 149 are a selected subset: joint support is checked (especially rare positive negations and morphology–valence combinations), and words outside warranted support get descriptive-only status. Calibration is frozen independently of marking and stereotype outcomes, and restricted ("cut") feedback is primary.
- *Does a judge add anything beyond word frequency?* Test: held-out prediction of human commonness from frequency, familiarity, valence and morphology alone against the same predictors plus judges, with every calibration step fitted inside each fold. Improvement is required overall and within valence and morphological strata. Passing shows incremental prediction of *human perceived* commonness, not information about actual prevalence.
- *Warrant, specific to each use.* Holdouts by morphological family, semantic domain and support boundary, not only random items. Leave-one-family-out tests instrument dependence, not vocabulary transport. Possible memorization of published tables (Anderson, Ziano) compromises item holdouts and can't be ruled out by paraphrase. Before use in H2, L1 or L2, the plan fixes acceptable calibration error, interval coverage, residual bias by frequency, valence and morphology, and propagated downstream bias, and simulates null and meaningful effects, selected anchors, shared availability errors and failed transport. L1's primary estimand uses morphology-free comparisons instead (see L1); L2 needs morphology, stakes and act-type support.
- *Framings.* Adjective, behavioural descriptions (frozen, checked for equivalence blind to ratings, wording variation estimated) and an identical-wording accessibility manipulation are **instrument diagnostics**: they show a judge's susceptibility to framing and access, not humans'. Behavioural outputs stay auxiliary until a bridge to the human scale is validated. Exception (proposed 2026-10-09, see L1): for L1's ordinal within-pair estimand, description-based comparisons are primary data, since that estimand needs no bridge to the absolute scale. Absolute description ratings stay a model-judged indicator with no human anchor (see L2, "Morphological bias").
- *Circularity.* Judges can reintroduce it: textual prevalence and textual stereotypes may drive both judged commonness and production. Calibration to humans keeps any availability bias humans share. Neither consensus nor hierarchy removes it, so the shared-bias sensitivity analyses stay.
- *In the multiverse.* Measurement alternatives (human only; human plus judges) are compared first on the same 149 words; expanded word sets are admitted only within warranted support, as separate populations.
- *Engine (settled, Brett, 2026-10-09: "reuse acceptometer's Stan measurement model").* The commonness measurement module reuses the acceptometer's Stan model and validation machinery (`tools/llm-acceptability-judgments/`), after checking its construct-specific assumptions for commonness; this amends the brief's "brms or PyMC" for that module. Its posterior draws feed the PyMC joint model, which also implements restricted ("cut") feedback. The joint model stays in PyMC.
- *The region the panel doesn't cover (revised 2026-10-09: no new human judges; Brett: "It will have to be model judgments all the way").* Ziano et al.'s 149 come from Anderson's list, which excluded "extreme words, such as ferocious", so extreme traits (*violent*, *cruel*) lie outside the human anchor, and no human judges will extend it. Words outside the calibrated judges' warranted support are model-judged outside human-anchored support: in H2 they form their own population (the expanded vocabulary), labelled so and reported separately, never pooled with supported words. ESCS self-ratings check endorsement associations on overlapping extreme words, as a diagnostic, not a prevalence indicator. Floor compression is checked in the model judges' ratings.

**Model judges only (2026-10-09; Brett: "It will have to be model judgments all the way"; after a Codex review, `reviews/codex-models-only-2026-10-09/`).** No new human judges take part, so the blinding protocol, the judge roles, the coding interface, and Brett's cross-check rows and reading audit are withdrawn, and the fingerprint-only rule for coder materials lapses. Model materials (prompts, items, randomization rules, model versions, exclusions and the reporting order) are frozen and published before any model judging, as preregistration, and every judgment runs in a fresh context that doesn't contain the hypotheses. Model judges may carry linguistic theory about negation and markedness into their judgments; descriptions reduce lexical cueing, and each family's word-versus-description gap is reported as a diagnostic.

*Overall warrant.* Human-supported conclusions concern Ziano et al.'s panel's perceived commonness of words, within demonstrated support. **H1 and the frequency control.** Gelman & Hill (2007, §9.7, p. 188): "it is generally not a good idea to control for variables measured after the treatment." If word frequency is partly a consequence of trait rarity, it's an intermediate outcome, and §9.8 cautions against regressing on intermediate outcomes to estimate mediation. Frequency is modelled jointly as a path, but the production equation still conditions on it, so modelling it jointly doesn't by itself avoid the problem: what an estimate means depends on the predictive averaging, either holding frequency fixed or integrating over its rarity- and marking-dependent distribution, and each estimand must say which (see "H2 details"; corrected 2026-10-09 after the Codex review of H1, `reviews/codex-h1-2026-10-09/`). This supersedes the "(b) the change when log frequency is added" reading in the open decision above.

**D1: design simulation (no outcome data).** Fake-data simulation in the sense of the *Bayesian Workflow* paper (Gelman et al. 2020, §4.1, p. 16). Two checks:

1. *Parameter recovery:* simulate from the joint model with known coefficients, fit it, and compare.
2. *Mechanism check:* simulate stereotype production from strand A's process (perceivers selecting high-PPV attributes from finite samples), add a word-frequency channel with accessibility and stakes effects, and fit the joint model. Does it recover the rarity signature from a process it doesn't share?

Conditions: word frequency as the only rarity indicator; word frequency plus one prevalence indicator at several reliabilities; plausible effect sizes; item, group and participant counts matching the real datasets once verified. D1's contraction and convergence criteria describe how the design performs under the assumed models; they don't establish structural identification (Codex review, 2026-10-09). Reported per coefficient: interval coverage, bias, interval width, and sign-error rate and exaggeration ratio (Gelman & Carlin 2014). The results decide whether a prevalence indicator is essential for each coefficient. Brett makes that call with D1's output in hand, before the plan is FIXED. Simulated data is used only to evaluate the design. It's never analysed as evidence and never stands in for a dataset that should come from a source.

**L1: which pole of an opposition carries the negator (pair level; lexical; settled 2026-10-09 after a Gelman pass and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-lexical-2026-10-09/`; human judging was withdrawn the same day, so L1 runs on model judges).** The hypothesis concerns "the marked pole of evaluative oppositions" (brief, lines 14–15), so its marking claim is first a pair-level one: in a root/negation opposition (*selfish/unselfish*, *kind/unkind*), the negated member names the rarer pole. The form-frequency principle concerns usage frequency within an opposition (Haspelmath & Karjus 2017, principle (3)), not prevalence among people, so L1 tests a reduced form that needs an auxiliary bridge: prevalence asymmetries must produce usage asymmetries, and stakes can break that bridge. If L1 fails, the bridge or the coding link may be at fault, and L1 can't say which. Haspelmath & Karjus call the effect "relatively weak" and place it in diachronic change (section 8), which gives no expected effect size for English, so the threshold is justified separately.

- *Frame.* All root/negation pairs (contrary prefixes and *non-*) whose members are both eligible person-descriptive adjectives, detected by rule and hand-checked, with sense-level eligibility and exclusions frozen before any commonness is collected or joined to morphology; pairs that can't be interpreted stay in a reported table. Pairs whose root isn't eligible are described, not used for the estimand. Negated words with no extant root (see Variables) define no pair, so the primary estimand covers extant-root pairs only; that restricts the population rather than biasing the comparison within it. They're tallied by valence and morphology. An exploratory stratum pairs each with the concept of its opposite, chosen by a frozen rule (model families, from the frozen sense) blind to commonness and frequency; any disputed choice runs under each alternative opposite; the stratum's comparisons are made by model judges only, so it adds no human time, and it's reported separately, never pooled into the primary contrast. Whether such roots died because they named an unremarkable default is a historical hypothesis this design doesn't test. Participial pairs (*shaken/unshaken*) follow the word list's eligibility rules, carry a participial indicator, and are also reported separately, since participial *un-* attaches freely. The list assembled from intuition is dropped: *unbeaten* and *undefeated* describe records, not person traits (UWA's own example of rare positive ability information is "winning a competition"). Hampson, Goldberg & John (1987) supply a second, probably overlapping frame once obtained; Hofstee (1995), a direct precedent reported by the prevalence search, is adjudicated once read.
- *Why word-based commonness can't carry L1.* If judges rate negated words as rarer by δ, the predictor shifts by +δ exactly when the negated member is positive and by −δ when it's negative, so the bias produces L1's predicted association under a null. Valence adjustment doesn't remove it, and a sensitivity grid makes conclusions conditional without identifying them. The bias can run either way: a marked form may seem rarer, or a judge may read *un-X* as 'not X' and rate it as the complement of a rare *X*.
- *Primary estimand: morphology-free and ordinal.* For each pair, which concept is judged more common in the population the commonness prompts fix, when both poles are given as behavioural descriptions that avoid the words and their roots (responses: either pole, about equal, can't say). The estimand is a protocol contrast, θ_m: the difference, between pairs whose positive pole is negated and the rest, in the probability that a model judge from the frozen panel, in one comparison under the fixed protocol, judges the positive pole the rarer (revised 2026-10-09 from a log odds ratio, whose scale depends on which judges and random effects are in the model, `reviews/codex-d2-spec-2026-10-09/`, and from a blind human coder's contrast, since no human judges take part, `reviews/codex-models-only-2026-10-09/`). It describes the frozen model protocol: it can confirm or contradict a registered prediction about θ_m, but bears on human perceived commonness only under a stated transport assumption. The rarity account predicts a positive contrast, and pairs whose negated member is positive are the ones that separate it from a valence account. It's estimated for the process that generates such pairs (primary, with the pair-generating population stated) and for the frame's own pairs (descriptive), as in D2. Because reading covaries with pair type, θ_m is also reported within reading strata (contrary pairs, contradictory pairs), with counts first and only where both pair types occur in a stratum, and the pooled θ_m is split into within-reading contrasts and reading composition. For exact complements the comparison reduces to whether the root is the majority state. Reading may itself result from lexicalization, so conditioning on it defines the construct; L1's claim stays descriptive. Ordinal comparisons can't recover a slope on logit commonness, and none is claimed. This amends "Framings": for this estimand, description-based judgments are primary data.
- *Descriptions and judges.* Two independent wordings per pole, written from the frozen dictionary sense (source fixed with the plan) by frozen prompts in two model families, checked for equivalence by model checks, so wording effects can be separated from concept differences. Each pole is described in its frozen sense's reading (revised 2026-10-09 after a side agent's note and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-reading-2026-10-09/`; the earlier rule described every negated word as a contrary, which would have rendered the contradictory, mostly positive negated words that separate the accounts as rare traits). Readings are screened before any description is written: model families judge, under the frozen sense, whether someone could satisfy neither description and whether *un-X* paraphrases as the complement of *X* or as an opposite. The morphological default guides the screening without deciding it: productive formations on participial and *-able* bases tend to be contradictories, often positive ones, and lexicalized adjectival *un-* and *iN-* on positive bases tend to be contraries (Horn 1989, §5.1, after Zimmer 1964). Contradictory negations are described as the complement of the root's description, contraries as traits in their own right. A word whose reading is disputed by any screen gets both descriptions and runs under both readings as a multiverse dimension, so no single judgment can narrow a word to one reading. Complement descriptions show grammatical negation ("doesn't ..."), so model judges also compare them with positive paraphrases of the same complement where one exists, as an instrument diagnostic. Model judges compare every pair under both wordings, each comparison in a fresh context with order and side randomized, intermixed with filler oppositions (semantic antonym pairs among eligible adjectives, from WordNet's antonym relation, version fixed with the plan), which also give a reference set. Shared error across families is modelled, and D2b stresses shared errors in writing and judging descriptions, including reversed bias. The result describes model-judged relative commonness, not accuracy about actual prevalence.
- *Secondary, conditional specification.* The word-based slope on the poles' logit commonness difference, with the valence difference and morphological-process terms (valence adjustment linear and flexible), reported across the morphological-bias grid with its tipping point (the δ at which the interval reaches 0).
- *Model size.* The frame will be small, so domains enter as varying intercepts only; domain-varying slopes and many process terms would mostly reflect their priors.
- *What counts against.* As for H2: meaningful support if the 90% interval for the protocol contrast lies wholly above a threshold, evidence against an effect that large if it lies wholly below, otherwise inconclusive. The threshold is justified and translated into probabilities before any comparison is collected. Evidence against a meaningful effect isn't evidence of absence.

**L2: which negative concepts get negated forms (word level; lexical; a conjecture; revised 2026-10-09 after the Codex review, `reviews/codex-lexical-2026-10-09/`).** If lexicalization follows payoff importance and short forms follow frequency of use (form-frequency correspondence; Haspelmath & Karjus 2017), then among negative person-descriptive adjectives, formal negation should cluster among low-stakes words (*impolite*, *untidy*, *unpunctual*) and simple forms among high-stakes ones (*cruel*, *violent*). The examples are illustrations, not data. Extremity, rarity and stakes probably move together among negative traits, so the rarity coefficient given stakes may be weakly identified; overlap is checked and reported before any coefficient is interpreted. Within negative words, valence extremity is a linear function of valence, so it can't be a stakes indicator separate from the valence term; L2's stakes rest on arousal, whose reliability as a stakes measure is an assumption, stated and varied, and which needs construct validation before it supports a stakes claim.

- *Morphological bias.* The bias that blocks word-based L1 contaminates every word-level coefficient linking commonness and negation (L2, the joint model's marking equation, H1's marking coefficient), since the shift falls exactly on the negated words. So word-level analyses add description-based commonness from the model judges (both wordings) as a separate indicator beside word-based commonness, and their conclusions are stated conditional on bounds on δ. *No human bridge (2026-10-09; Brett settled one, then withdrew all new human judging: "It will have to be model judgments all the way"):* absolute description ratings have no human anchor, so they stay a model-judged indicator with their own shared-bias bounds, and no absolute-scale bridge to the panel is assumed.
- *Act type (adopted for L2, Brett, 2026-10-09).* Each word is coded for act type, naming acts done (commission) or an expected act not done (omission), and act type enters the L2 model as an item-level covariate. In the side session's French coding, the L2 illustrations split along it (*cruel* and *violent* coded 'doing', *désordonné* 'not doing'; per its brief), so without it a stakes effect could be an act-type effect. Omission may be constitutive of a derived concept (*un-X* often names failing to do X-acts), so act type may be partly a consequence of the negation. It's coded from the morphology-free descriptions (as for H1; see "H1 details"), which reduces lexical cueing without making it causally prior; adjusted and unadjusted contrasts are reported on the same population under the alternative causal diagrams required for H1, and coefficient movement isn't read as bias removed. The coding source (the side session's multi-family instrument, `Project-Management/notes/2026-10-08-marked-pole-french-v2/prompts/code.txt`, or human coders) is fixed with the plan.
- *Model.* Categorical outcome (simple; contrary prefix; *non-*; *-less*), affixes partially pooled within the contrary class, varying intercepts for morphological family and semantic domain; prior and posterior predictive checks (negation share by valence bin, affix counts, off-diagonal counts).
- **What counts against the marking claim (settled, Brett, 2026-10-09: "pairs plus words"; amended the same day for model-only judging, the amendment pending Brett).** The earlier wording ("If marking follows stakes and not prevalence, the hypothesis's single-rarity claim fails") is withdrawn: a stakes association alone doesn't establish it, since a rarity effect may coexist, be poorly measured, or lack overlap (Codex review). The hypothesis commits to both levels, each with a decision rule fixed now. With no human judges, both rules classify model-judged quantities, and their bearing on human perceived commonness rests on a stated transport assumption. *Pair level:* the registered prediction that θ_m is positive, classified by L1's rule above. *Word level, for the common-cause account behind H1:* the marking submodel's average predictive comparison of within-valence commonness on the probability of negation (the rarity account predicts a negative value), estimated twice, once with word-based commonness from the Ziano-calibrated judges across the pre-specified δ range, and once with description-based model commonness across its own shared-bias bounds, on the same vocabulary, contrast and threshold. It counts as meaningful support only if both place the 90% interval wholly below −T throughout the admissible bias and transport specifications, and as evidence against an effect that large only if both place it wholly above −T throughout; otherwise it's inconclusive, and any disagreement is diagnosed. This agreement rule is an operational decision rule, not validation or independent corroboration. If overlap with stakes fails a pre-specified diagnostic, the result is inconclusive by design. T, like L1's threshold, is justified before FIXED. Fixing both in advance stops a failure at one level being reassigned to the other after the fact; the levels share the word list and the commonness instrument, so they're commitments, not independent tests.

## Variables

**Senses of "negative" (settled 2026-10-07; see `DECISIONS.md`).** Four senses stay separate, each with its own variable or none:

- **Valence**: the attribute is evaluated as bad. Warriner rating. In this plan, "negative attribute" and "positive attribute" mean valence and nothing else.
- **Formal negation**: the word contains a negator. Coded by type, because the types differ in meaning: contrary prefixes (*un-*, *in-/im-/il-/ir-*, *dis-*), *non-*, and privative *-less*. Record the base's valence too (*flawless* negates a negative base, *flaw*, and yields a positive word). Hand-check detections (*uniform*, *discuss*). Words whose root has no extant use, such as *uncouth*, *unkempt* and *unruly* (Horn 1989, §5.1, lists them among "un- adjectives with no—or no extant—relevant positive counterpart") or *inept* and *ungainly* (CGEL ch. 19 §5.5, fn. 44), are coded as negated with no extant root, a separate category like opaque Latinate *in-*, partially pooled with the contrary prefixes; word-level analyses run with and without them.
- **Order markedness**: Ingram, Hand & Maciejewski's (2016) markedness probability, where their 154 pairs cover the word. A behavioural measure, not a morphological one.
- **Scalar polarity**: the less-of pole of a dimension (*short*, *few*). Valence-neutral; not used as a markedness measure for H1.

**Feature absence** (UWA's presence/absence coding, πneg = 1 − πpos) is a modelling choice in strand A, not a property of words. Affixal negation isn't treated as absence: *un-* on an evaluative adjective usually gives a contrary (someone can be neither friendly nor unfriendly), but productive formations on participial and *-able* bases usually give contradictories, often positive ones (Horn 1989, §5.1, after Zimmer 1964), so L1 codes the reading per sense. If strand A is extended to carry marking, an attribute needs three values (positive, neither, negative), closer to UWA's range principle than to their binary coding.
