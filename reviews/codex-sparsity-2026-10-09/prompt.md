1. Kilgarriff (2005, CLLT 1(2): 263–275): "Language users never choose words randomly ... the null hypothesis will never be true. Moreover, where there is enough data, we shall (almost) always be able to establish that it is not true ... the fact that a relation between two phenomena is demonstrably non-random, does not support the inference that it is not arbitrary." His typology: Random, Arbitrary, Motivated, Predictable. Draft: the plan doesn't test zero nulls (threshold evidence rules, multiverse labels), so his first point bites only if the thresholds are set casually; they're placeholders now and must be justified substantively. His deeper point bites fully: lexical properties (frequency, length, register, age of acquisition, etymology, valence, morphology) are all intercorrelated, so an association between rarity and marking or production can be arbitrary in his sense. Statistics can't move us from A to M; the plan's answers are measured confounders, D1/D2 confound scenarios, descriptive framing of L1, predictive framing of H1, and convergence across independent tests (pair level, word level, production), with the residual alternatives stated.

2. Dunning (1993, CL 19(1): 61–74): "asymptotic normality assumptions have often been used unjustifiably ... This assumption of normal distribution limits the ability to analyze rare events. Unfortunately rare events do make up a large fraction of real text"; "mutual information estimates based directly on counts are subject to overestimation when the counts involved are small, and z-scores substantially overestimate the significance of rare events." Draft: the plan uses exact likelihoods and partial pooling (no asymptotic tests), and D2 calibrates at small sizes, so his specific complaint is handled. One real gap: log word frequency enters the joint model with one homoskedastic residual SD (d1.py, sig_f), but for rare words it's measured with much larger, count-driven error (roughly 1/sqrt(count), more with burstiness), exactly along the rarity dimension. Proposed fix: model frequency counts with a count likelihood (corpus size as exposure, negative-binomial dispersion, document dispersion where the source reports it), or give each word a known SE from its count.

3. Words with no extant root: uncouth, unkempt, ungainly, unruly, unwieldy. Horn (1989, §5.1) lists "un- adjectives with no—or no extant—relevant positive counterpart whatsoever: unabashed, unassuming, unbending, uncouth, unflagging, unheard-of, unkempt, unparalleled, unprecedented, unruly, unscathed, untouched, untoward", adding that the majority of such forms, "many of which are evaluatively positive", are productive un + V + participle formations. CGEL (ch. 19 §5.5, fn. 44): "inept, non-commital, ungainly: here there is no word consisting of the base without the prefix". Draft: (a) code them as formally negated but in a separate no-extant-root category (like opaque Latinate in-), partially pooled with the contrary prefixes, run with and without them; (b) L1's frame currently drops them because it needs an eligible root, but root survival may itself depend on rarity (a root naming the unremarkable default, such as kempt or ruly, may fall out of use while the negated form survives), so dropping them is selection related to the hypothesis; since L1 compares concepts described without the words, include them by pairing each negated word with the concept of its modern opposite, described from its frozen sense, as a separate stratum; this also adds positive negated words (unassuming, unflagging, unparalleled, unscathed), the scarce discriminating type; (c) they show coding outliving the frequency asymmetry (the marked form became the common word), a caution against reading present-day frequency as the asymmetry that shaped the form; (d) D2b runs root availability in both directions.
