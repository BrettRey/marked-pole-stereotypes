- *What H1 asks.* An exploratory, predictive question: does formal negation improve held-out prediction of word choice beyond within-valence rarity, valence, word frequency and measured word properties? Prediction is evaluated by holding out **words or morphological families**, not participants (participant holdouts leave the word-level problem untouched). The conditional association is also reported as a posterior-predictive comparison averaged over a common-support population of words and groups, with the competing vocabulary fixed and the treatment of frequency (held fixed or integrated over) stated. Predictive improvement remains compatible with confounding, so H1 isn't read as evidence for or against the common-cause account.

**Multiverse (settled, Brett, 2026-10-09: "Gelman would usually say use them all. We take a multiverse approach"; design revised the same day after a Codex review, `reviews/codex-multiverse-2026-10-09/`).** Where several analytic options are defensible, all are run as a pre-specified multiverse (Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman & Vanpaemel 2016) and reported across, instead of one being chosen. Pre-specification constrains the fitting choices; the rules for ordering and emphasis in reporting are fixed in advance too, since otherwise emphasis after the fact would be its own forking path.

*What varies, and how it is reported, in three kinds:*

- **Alternative models of one estimand** (the multiverse proper): prevalence indicators (Ziano et al.'s commonness alone; with Ziano self- and other-ratings as separate auxiliary constructs; with the ESCS self-ratings as auxiliary endorsement data, never as a prevalence indicator; with the new ratings, adjective, behavioural or both, if collected); availability (a grid of shared familiarity bias and of direct morphological bias in the prevalence ratings; familiarity from Condon, Coughlin & Weston 2022); valence bias of the prevalence measure (a grid, for the rarity-versus-valence contrast); valence adjustment (linear and flexible); measurement-invariance and transport restrictions (with and without); feedback from outcomes to inferred rarity (joint, and restricted, "cut"); H1 controls (none, register, act type, both); response normalization (strict and lenient, both frozen after UWA Study 1); priors (reference, and substantively calibrated identifying priors, not merely widened ones).
- **Different estimands, reported side by side, never tallied together:** frequency held fixed and frequency integrated over, computed from the same posterior where possible.
- **Different target populations, reported as separate strata:** word sets (Ziano's 149; ESCS-covered; all eligible; Anderson only; the expanded list) and datasets (UWA Study 2, Nicolas et al., Ingendahl et al.'s Irish subsample). Each word set changes the population and the competing vocabulary, so it isn't a robustness check of another.

The categorical choice likelihood is fixed, not varied: independent negative-binomial counts contradict fixed response budgets and competition among words (see "H2 details"), and no sampling rationale supports them. Only admissible combinations are run; unavailable indicators and unsupported scale bridges aren't alternatives. For the lexical models (L1, L2), which are cheap, the relevant dimensions are crossed in full. For the joint production model, a fixed fractional-factorial or space-filling design covering the coupled assumptions (indicators × word sets × availability; availability × valence bias; H1 controls × measurement reliability and feedback) and the plausible bias extremes is chosen by simulation (D1b, D1c) before any stereotype outcomes are opened. Convergence criteria, retry rules, and the display of failed fits are registered; robustness is claimed only over the evaluated design.

*Model expansion where uncertainty is measured; separate fits where it isn't.* Measured uncertainties go inside one expanded model: respondent-level measurement with rater effects, instrument-specific loadings, uncertain act-type codes, partially pooled affix effects within the contrary-prefix class, and regularized nonlinearities. Assumptions the data can't adjudicate (shared bias restrictions, feedback policies) stay as separate sensitivity fits; averaging over them would hide the identifying assumptions inside prior weights.

*Reporting.* Every specification's estimate and interval, in a fixed pre-registered order, stratified by dataset, estimand and word set, on a common contrast scale (the reference scaling of "1 SD of rarity" is frozen across word sets where comparable, not re-standardized within each subset). The H2 evidence rule is applied to each admissible specification separately. Overall labels, fixed in advance: *uniform meaningful support* if every specification qualifies; *uniform evidence against an effect that large* if every one qualifies against; otherwise *specification-dependent*, with the inconclusive and conflicting branches shown. The share of specifications meeting a criterion is descriptive only, not a probability (duplicating grid points would change it). Direction is reported separately from magnitude. Datasets aren't pooled into any tally.

**Commonness judges (replacing the paid rating study, which isn't funded; design settled 2026-10-09 after a Gelman pass and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-judges-2026-10-09/`; engine amendment pending Brett).**

- *The human anchor.* Ziano et al.'s raw commonness ratings (149 traits, about 80 raters each), modelled at the respondent level, ordinal, with rater effects and posterior predictive checks of category use and item disagreement. The OSF data structure and instructions are verified first. The anchor measures this panel's perceived commonness, not actual prevalence; Ziano's wording also mixes "how many people have it" with "how often it's displayed".
- *Judges.* Several model families, local first, versions and prompts frozen (population, timeframe, trait interpretation, scale). Logprob-based expected ratings and chat ratings are distinct instruments; excluded probability mass and tokenization are checked; repeated chats measure stochastic variation, not independent evidence. Correlated errors across families and prompt effects are modelled, since several families can share one systematic error. A small pilot comes before the full vocabulary.
- *Calibration and transport.* Judges are linked to the human latent on the 149 with partially pooled discrepancies, regularized nonlinearities, correlated instrument errors and a systematic-error floor. Carrying calibration to the other words isn't automatic, since the 149 are a selected subset: joint support is checked (especially rare positive negations and morphology–valence combinations), and words outside warranted support get descriptive-only status. Calibration is frozen independently of marking and stereotype outcomes, and restricted ("cut") feedback is primary.
- *Does a judge add anything beyond word frequency?* Test: held-out prediction of human commonness from frequency, familiarity, valence and morphology alone against the same predictors plus judges, with every calibration step fitted inside each fold. Improvement is required overall and within valence and morphological strata. Passing shows incremental prediction of *human perceived* commonness, not information about actual prevalence.
- *Warrant, specific to each use.* Holdouts by morphological family, semantic domain and support boundary, not only random items. Leave-one-family-out tests instrument dependence, not vocabulary transport. Possible memorization of published tables (Anderson, Ziano) compromises item holdouts and can't be ruled out by paraphrase. Before use in H2, L1 or L2, the plan fixes acceptable calibration error, interval coverage, residual bias by frequency, valence and morphology, and propagated downstream bias, and simulates null and meaningful effects, selected anchors, shared availability errors and failed transport. L1's primary estimand uses morphology-free comparisons instead (see L1); L2 needs morphology, stakes and act-type support.
- *Framings.* Adjective, behavioural descriptions (frozen, checked for equivalence blind to ratings, wording variation estimated) and an identical-wording accessibility manipulation are **instrument diagnostics**: they show a judge's susceptibility to framing and access, not humans'. Behavioural outputs stay auxiliary until a bridge to the human scale is validated. Exception (proposed 2026-10-09, see L1): for L1's ordinal within-pair estimand, description-based comparisons are primary data, since that estimand needs no bridge to the absolute scale.
- *Circularity.* Judges can reintroduce it: textual prevalence and textual stereotypes may drive both judged commonness and production. Calibration to humans keeps any availability bias humans share. Neither consensus nor hierarchy removes it, so the shared-bias sensitivity analyses stay.
- *In the multiverse.* Measurement alternatives (human only; human plus judges) are compared first on the same 149 words; expanded word sets are admitted only within warranted support, as separate populations.
- *Engine (settled, Brett, 2026-10-09: "reuse acceptometer's Stan measurement model").* The commonness measurement module reuses the acceptometer's Stan model and validation machinery (`tools/llm-acceptability-judgments/`), after checking its construct-specific assumptions for commonness; this amends the brief's "brms or PyMC" for that module. Its posterior draws feed the PyMC joint model, which also implements restricted ("cut") feedback. The joint model stays in PyMC.
- *Human judges for the region the panel doesn't cover (settled 2026-10-09 after a Codex review, `reviews/codex-extreme-anchor-2026-10-09/`).* Ziano et al.'s 149 come from Anderson's list, which excluded "extreme words, such as ferocious", so extreme traits (*violent*, *cruel*) lie outside the anchor. The second human coder (blind to the hypotheses and to model outputs) is the primary human judge there; Brett, who can't be blind, contributes a pre-specified sensitivity row; two or three further blind volunteer judges are added if they can be found. Each judge rates commonness, with Ziano's wording and scale, for (a) a linking set of about 60–80 of Ziano's traits spanning panel commonness, valence, morphology, semantic domain and boundary items, and (b) an extension set of about 100–150 traits drawn at random from the frozen eligible list, oversampling extreme negatives while keeping near-boundary negatives, positive extremes and other unsupported combinations, with morphology and provisional act type balanced and inclusion probabilities recorded; nothing is selected on stereotype outcomes or model commonness. Final sizes are set by simulating calibration precision and downstream H2 bias. Linking and extension items are intermixed in a fresh random order per judge with strata hidden; commonness is collected before act-type coding, in separate sessions; commonness ratings are kept independent, not adjudicated. Each human judge is modelled separately, with judge-specific thresholds and discrepancies estimated on the overlap. Linking places a judge on the panel's scale within the panel's support; it can't identify a discrepancy that all judges share in the extreme region. So expanded-vocabulary H2 estimates carry a qualified warrant: "conditional on transport of judge-to-panel calibration and stated bounds on extreme-region shared bias", distinct from validated panel support, and reported separately. Floor compression is checked (if extreme traits all get minimum ratings, more ratings won't recover their order).

**H1 and the frequency control.** Gelman & Hill (2007, §9.7, p. 188): "it is generally not a good idea to control for variables measured after the treatment." If word frequency is partly a consequence of trait rarity, it's an intermediate outcome, and §9.8 cautions against regressing on intermediate outcomes to estimate mediation. Frequency is modelled jointly as a path, but the production equation still conditions on it, so modelling it jointly doesn't by itself avoid the problem: what an estimate means depends on the predictive averaging, either holding frequency fixed or integrating over its rarity- and marking-dependent distribution, and each estimand must say which (see "H2 details"; corrected 2026-10-09 after the Codex review of H1, `reviews/codex-h1-2026-10-09/`). This supersedes the "(b) the change when log frequency is added" reading in the open decision above.

**D1: design simulation (no outcome data).** Fake-data simulation in the sense of the *Bayesian Workflow* paper (Gelman et al. 2020, §4.1, p. 16). Two checks:
**Strand A, extension A5: unequal group sizes.** UWA say their account "does not require assumptions about minority status or group size" (Cognitive Principle section), but their simulation fixes 10 groups of 100. Vary the target group's share at fixed total N and report UWA's three outcomes by share. Comparison point, not a shared mechanism: the cultural Red King effect (O'Connor 2019, *Origins of Unfairness*, ch. 6), where minority disadvantage comes from learning speed in strategic games. Optional A6: three-valued attributes (positive, neither, negative), per Variables.

**L1: which pole of an opposition carries the negator (pair level; lexical; proposed 2026-10-09 after a Gelman pass and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-lexical-2026-10-09/`; the human workload is pending Brett).** The hypothesis concerns "the marked pole of evaluative oppositions" (brief, lines 14–15), so its marking claim is first a pair-level one: in a root/negation opposition (*selfish/unselfish*, *kind/unkind*), the negated member names the rarer pole. The form-frequency principle concerns usage frequency within an opposition (Haspelmath & Karjus 2017, principle (3)), not prevalence among people, so L1 tests a reduced form that needs an auxiliary bridge: prevalence asymmetries must produce usage asymmetries, and stakes can break that bridge. If L1 fails, the bridge or the coding link may be at fault, and L1 can't say which. Haspelmath & Karjus call the effect "relatively weak" and place it in diachronic change (section 8), which gives no expected effect size for English, so the threshold is justified separately.

- *Frame.* All root/negation pairs (contrary prefixes and *non-*) whose members are both eligible person-descriptive adjectives, detected by rule and hand-checked, with sense-level eligibility and exclusions frozen before any commonness is collected or joined to morphology; pairs that can't be interpreted stay in a reported table. Pairs whose root isn't eligible are described, not used for the estimand. Negated words with no extant root (see Variables) define no pair, so the primary estimand covers extant-root pairs only; that restricts the population rather than biasing the comparison within it. They're tallied by valence and morphology. An exploratory stratum pairs each with the concept of its opposite, chosen by a frozen rule (model families, from the frozen sense) blind to commonness and frequency; any disputed choice runs under each alternative opposite; the stratum's comparisons are made by model judges only, so it adds no human time, and it's reported separately, never pooled into the primary contrast. Whether such roots died because they named an unremarkable default is a historical hypothesis this design doesn't test. Participial pairs (*shaken/unshaken*) follow the word list's eligibility rules, carry a participial indicator, and are also reported separately, since participial *un-* attaches freely. The list assembled from intuition is dropped: *unbeaten* and *undefeated* describe records, not person traits (UWA's own example of rare positive ability information is "winning a competition"). Hampson, Goldberg & John (1987) supply a second, probably overlapping frame once obtained; Hofstee (1995), a direct precedent reported by the prevalence search, is adjudicated once read.
- *Why word-based commonness can't carry L1.* If judges rate negated words as rarer by δ, the predictor shifts by +δ exactly when the negated member is positive and by −δ when it's negative, so the bias produces L1's predicted association under a null. Valence adjustment doesn't remove it, and a sensitivity grid makes conclusions conditional without identifying them. The bias can run either way: a marked form may seem rarer, or a judge may read *un-X* as 'not X' and rate it as the complement of a rare *X*.
- *Primary estimand: morphology-free and ordinal.* For each pair, which concept is judged more common in the population the commonness prompts fix, when both poles are given as behavioural descriptions that avoid the words and their roots (responses: either pole, about equal, can't say). The estimand is a protocol contrast: the difference, between pairs whose positive pole is negated and the rest, in the probability that the blind coder, in one comparison under the fixed protocol, judges the positive pole the rarer (revised 2026-10-09 from a log odds ratio, whose scale depends on which judge and random effects are in the model; `reviews/codex-d2-spec-2026-10-09/`). The rarity account predicts a positive contrast, and pairs whose negated member is positive are the ones that separate it from a valence account. Model judges give the same contrast for their own responses, a different quantity unless bridged to the coder's. It's estimated for the process that generates such pairs (primary) and for the frame's own pairs (descriptive), as in D2. Because reading covaries with pair type, θ is also reported within reading strata (contrary pairs, contradictory pairs), with counts first and only where both pair types occur in a stratum, and the pooled θ is split into within-reading contrasts and reading composition. For exact complements the comparison reduces to whether the root is the majority state. Reading may itself result from lexicalization, so conditioning on it defines the construct; L1's claim stays descriptive. Ordinal comparisons can't recover a slope on logit commonness, and none is claimed. This amends "Framings": for this estimand, description-based judgments are primary data.
- *Descriptions and judges.* Two independent wordings per pole, written from the frozen dictionary sense (source fixed with the plan) by frozen prompts in two model families, checked for equivalence by model checks (all) and the blind second coder (a random sample), so wording effects can be separated from concept differences. Each pole is described in its frozen sense's reading (revised 2026-10-09 after a side agent's note and a Codex review, `reviews/codex-reading-2026-10-09/`; the earlier rule described every negated word as a contrary, which would have rendered the contradictory, mostly positive negated words that separate the accounts as rare traits). Readings are screened before any description is written: model families judge, under the frozen sense, whether someone could satisfy neither description and whether *un-X* paraphrases as the complement of *X* or as an opposite. The morphological default guides the screening without deciding it: productive formations on participial and *-able* bases tend to be contradictories, often positive ones, and lexicalized adjectival *un-* and *iN-* on positive bases tend to be contraries (Horn 1989, §5.1, after Zimmer 1964). Contradictory negations are described as the complement of the root's description, contraries as traits in their own right. A word whose reading is disputed by any screen, or by Brett's audit of a pre-specified sample balanced across morphology and pair type, gets both descriptions and runs under both readings as a multiverse dimension, so no single judgment, Brett's included, can narrow a word to one reading. Complement descriptions show grammatical negation ("doesn't ..."), so model judges also compare them with positive paraphrases of the same complement where one exists, as an instrument diagnostic. The blind second coder compares every pair once, with wording, order and side randomized, intermixed with filler oppositions (semantic antonym pairs among eligible adjectives, from WordNet's antonym relation, version fixed with the plan; they also give a reference set) so the stratum is hidden. The coder's tasks run in separate sessions in this order: description comparisons, word-based commonness, act-type coding, and last the equivalence spot-check, which shows descriptions beside their words. A second blind judge, if one can be found, compares a stratified subset that includes every pair with a positive negated member; the subset's size is fixed from the precision wanted on disagreement and wording effects. Brett's comparisons, if any, are a sensitivity row. Model judges compare every pair under both wordings, as instruments. The result is limited reproducibility of perceived relative commonness, not accuracy about actual prevalence.
- *Secondary, conditional specification.* The word-based slope on the poles' logit commonness difference, with the valence difference and morphological-process terms (valence adjustment linear and flexible), reported across the morphological-bias grid with its tipping point (the δ at which the interval reaches 0).

- **Measure exposure separately from inference.** Before recruitment, screen neutrally for familiarity with Brett’s relevant work, collaborations and prior discussions. After the last task, collect an open-ended hypothesis guess, confidence, when the idea arose, and any searching or outside discussion. A correct guess doesn’t establish exposure; an incorrect guess doesn’t establish blindness. “Measured blindness” overstates what self-report identifies.

- **Specify contamination decisions beforehand.** Known exposure before coding should trigger replacement for the blind-primary role. For exposure during coding, record timing, retain original responses, and report affected tasks separately. If no unexposed replacement exists, explicitly report that the planned blind-primary evidence is unavailable. Don’t automatically exclude an accurate guess: it could arise from attentive task performance.
