Frequency may also be partly downstream of stereotyping, which makes it a poor control. Candidate structure: (a) markedness without the frequency control, as the common-cause test; (b) the change when log frequency is added, read as how much of (a) frequency accounts for; (c) positive words containing a negator (*flawless*, *blameless*), where the marked-pole and valence accounts predict differently. Caveat: *flawless* is privative *-less* on a noun, and its antonym *flawed* is also derived, so it isn't clearly the marked member of its pair.
    11	- Strand A predictions (from the brief): fresh-sample PPV at chance (1/K) with no true differences; high valence agreement and low content agreement across perceivers with no true differences, content agreement rising with true differences.
    12	- **Open decision (raised 2026-10-07 by a reviewing agent; Brett to settle before FIXED): the brief's last strand A step, mapping how large true differences must be for simulated content agreement to reach observed stereotype consensus.** The observed data don't measure what the simulation's perceivers do. UWA asked for stereotypes "you know that exist in society", and Ingendahl et al. for what "society attributes"; agreement in such answers mostly reflects shared cultural knowledge, which the consensus split leaves out. Matching the two would put all observed agreement down to true group differences. Adding a transmission parameter doesn't rescue the mapping, because one agreement statistic can't separate true differences from transmission. Recommended: drop the quantitative mapping and keep the qualitative result (sampling alone yields consensus on valence but not on content, so observed content consensus needs true differences, cultural transmission, or both). Reframe only if a dataset turns up that elicits perceivers' own beliefs.

    16	- *Estimand.* The primary quantity is a model-based marginal association: the posterior-predictive change in production for a 1 SD difference in within-valence rarity, averaging over word frequency **and marking** under the joint model. Conditional coefficients (holding frequency and marking) are reported second. Orientation: z measures prevalence (higher = more common), so H2 predicts negative values.

    39	**What the model can and can't identify (settled by D1's pilot, 2026-10-07).** Any prevalence measure partly lines up with valence, and the data can't tell whether that overlap is real (common traits tend to be desirable) or the measure's own bias (people say desirable traits fit them). So the latent is *within-valence* rarity, and each equation carries a *total* valence term. Identifiable: at the same valence, are rarer traits more often marked (L1, L2) or more often produced as stereotypes (H2)? Is marking associated with production beyond rarity (H1)? **Not identifiable without an extra assumption:** does valence matter once rarity is known? That is the "rarity rather than valence" half of the marked-pole claim, and close to UWA's own argument. The plan therefore claims no rarity-versus-valence contrast. **Settled (Brett, 2026-10-09):** route (b), two prevalence measures whose valence biases differ in a known direction (self-endorsement inflates desirable traits; judged prevalence in others should do so less), where such measures exist; otherwise route (a), one measure with a sensitivity analysis over a stated range of its valence bias. 1. **Keep the split, but don’t claim both levels test Haspelmath–Karjus.** The brief commits to an opposition-level claim plus a broader common-cause explanation ([brief:14–15](notes/project-brief.md:14)); its operational H1 is word-level production ([brief:67–71](notes/project-brief.md:67)). The plan conflates pair-level L1 with word-level marking ([plan:34](analysis_plan.md:34), [101](analysis_plan.md:101)). Crucially, the supplied form-frequency principle concerns **usage**, not trait prevalence. Neither lexical test directly tests it. Both require an additional prevalence-to-usage hypothesis; stakes could disrupt that bridge. Section 8’s weak diachronic effect supplies neither an expected English prevalence effect size nor justification for “visible only off the diagonal.”

2. **The bias argument is correct under its stated coding.** Let \(Y=1\) mean positive negated member and \(D\) mean positive-pole rarity minus negative-pole rarity. A negation-induced rarity bias gives \(D_{\mathrm{observed}}=D_{\mathrm{true}}+\delta(2Y-1)\), creating association under a null. Linear or flexible valence adjustment doesn’t remove this direct morphological contamination. Without restrictions on that bias, adjective-only L1 is unidentified. A sensitivity grid makes conclusions conditional; it doesn’t identify them. The word–description gap also includes semantic and framing differences, so it diagnoses instrument dependence rather than estimating \(\delta\) automatically.

   Description comparisons can support a **different L1 estimand**: whether the negated concept is judged less prevalent. They need no absolute-scale bridge, but ordinal comparisons cannot recover the proposed logit-prevalence-difference slope. This requires an explicit amendment to the auxiliary-only rule ([plan:84](analysis_plan.md:84)). Least defensible human work: one blinded judge compares **every pair**, with randomized order and “equal/uncertain” responses; a second blinded judge independently checks a stratified subset, including rare-positive pairs, using independently written, equivalence-checked descriptions. That establishes limited reproducibility, not accuracy about actual prevalence. Fix subset size from precision on disagreement and framing effects; Brett alone cannot provide blind validation. One wording per concept leaves description bias inseparable from concept differences.

3. **L2 rewording is fair only with an explicit reduction in scope.** The existing plan says stakes without prevalence defeats the “single-rarity claim” ([plan:103](analysis_plan.md:103)). A stakes association alone cannot establish that: a rarity effect may coexist, be poorly measured, or lack overlap. Preserve falsifiability by specifying which well-identified prevalence effects would count against the broader common-cause account. Assigning every inconvenient result solely to the word-level account would protect the original explanation without justification. Arousal and extremity also need construct validation as stakes indicators.

4. **Description coding doesn’t resolve act type’s causal position.** It reduces lexical cueing, but omission can remain constitutive of the derived concept. Morphology-free wording isn’t causally prior meaning. Use explicit alternative causal structures and adjusted/unadjusted contrasts on the same population, as H1 already requires ([plan:56–60](analysis_plan.md:56)). Don’t describe coefficient movement as bias removed.

5. **D2 needs selection and model failure.** Include eligibility-dependent selection, root availability, shared roots, near-equal poles, uncertain valence orientation, separation, sparse affix classes, correlated description errors, and floor compression. Simulate mechanisms outside the fitted model, not just parameter recovery. Report false meaningful-support decisions under systematic bias; Type M is undefined at zero. Evaluate standardized contrasts as well as slopes, with fixed scaling across specifications ([Multiverse](analysis_plan.md:63)).

6. **Simplify where the frame cannot support complexity.** Domain-varying slopes plus process effects may be largely prior-driven. A derived-only stratum cannot contribute to the primary pair estimand. Freeze sense-level eligibility and exclusions, retain uninterpretable pairs transparently, and distinguish interval-based evidence against a meaningful effect from evidence of absence.
