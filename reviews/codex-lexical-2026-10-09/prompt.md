Haspelmath & Karjus (2017, *Linguistics* 55(6)), quoted from the paper since it sits outside the repository. Principle (3): "When two minimally different grammatical patterns (i.e., patterns that form an opposition) occur with significantly different frequencies, the less frequent pattern tends to be overtly coded (or coded with more coding material), while the more frequent pattern tends to be zero-coded (or coded with less coding material)." Section 8: "the correspondence between form and frequency is implemented by diachronic mechanisms which tend to make frequent forms short, because frequent forms are more predictable than rare forms"; "The causal effect is relatively weak, so it cannot be seen in all languages ... (In most languages, all lexemes join the majority pattern, due to system pressure, cf. Haspelmath 2014.)" Their frequency is usage frequency, not prevalence in the world.

Context: L1 and L2 are the lexical tests of the rarity-to-marking link. Their outcome is morphology (formal negation by affix type), which every English speaker can see, so the only unseen ingredient is the prevalence measurement (model judges calibrated to Ziano et al.'s human panel, plus human judges for an extension set). Draft recommendations (main session's Gelman pass), for you to attack:

1. Two levels, two estimands, both kept and labelled. (a) Pair level: in a root/negation opposition (*selfish/unselfish*, *kind/unkind*), is the negated member the rarer pole? This is what "marked pole of evaluative oppositions" and the form-frequency principle (Haspelmath & Karjus 2017, (3)) speak to. (b) Word level: at the same valence, are rarer words more often negated? This is what the joint model's marking equation and H1's common-cause structure use. The plan currently mixes them: the L1 section is pair level, the marking submodel describes L1 "among positive words" at word level.
2. L1 sampling frame, frozen before any prevalence is joined: all root/negation pairs among eligible person-descriptive adjectives (rule-detected, hand-checked; both members eligible; a secondary stratum where only the derived member is eligible). Drop the intuition-assembled list (*unbeaten*, *undefeated* describe competitors' records, not person traits). Hampson, Goldberg & John (1987, 573 terms) as a second frame if obtained, though probably not an independent one (Anderson's list derives from Allport & Odbert 1936; Hampson's likely shares that stock, unverified). Hofstee (1995), which our literature search reports used such pairs (*unenvious/envious*; unread, so unverified), is a direct precedent. Outcome per pair: whether the negated member is the positive one. Estimand: the slope on the poles' prevalence difference (logit scale), with the poles' valence difference and morphological-process terms in the model (valence adjustment linear and flexible, as in the multiverse), since a measure whose valence bias grows with extremity would otherwise pass that bias into the slope. The intercept mixes the valence preference of negation with the measure's valence bias and isn't interpreted.
3. Morphological bias is L1's central threat. If judges rate negated words as rarer by δ, the predictor shifts by +δ exactly when the negated member is positive and by −δ when it's negative, so the bias tracks the outcome and yields a spurious positive slope with no true effect. Mitigations: rate each pole both by its word and by a morphology-free behavioural description (written blind, equivalence-checked); treat the word-versus-description gap as an instrument diagnostic; report the L1 slope across the morphological-bias grid with its tipping point (the δ at which the interval reaches 0). The human judges' extension set includes both members of every L1 pair. Two costs: the description arm roughly doubles the human judges' L1 ratings (Brett and one blind coder, unpaid; about 160–230 commonness ratings each already planned), and the plan ("Framings") keeps behavioural outputs auxiliary until a bridge to the human scale is validated. Possible way out: L1 needs only the within-pair contrast (which pole is rarer), not absolute commonness, so a description-based pairwise comparison might carry L1 without that bridge.
4. Expect a small effect, visible only off the diagonal: Haspelmath & Karjus (section 8) call the form-frequency effect "relatively weak", with most items following the majority pattern through "system pressure". Of Ingram et al.'s (2016) 68 morphological pairs, 9 have a positive negated member, few of them person-descriptive. D2 (below) decides whether L1 is informative at the realized pair count; if not, it is reported descriptively with intervals, not dropped.
5. L2: among negative traits, extremity, rarity and stakes probably move together (*cruel*, *violent* are rare, high-stakes and simple; *untidy*, *impolite* common, low-stakes and negated), so the rarity-given-stakes coefficient may be weakly identified; check overlap. The form-frequency mechanism runs through usage frequency, which rises with stakes as well as falling with rarity, so L2 tests a rival lexicalization account at word level. Proposed rewording: a stakes effect bears on the word-level claim and the joint model's marking equation; the pair-level claim is tested by L1.
6. Act type may be partly created by the negation (*un-X* often names failing to do X-acts), so as a covariate of marking it can be a descendant of the outcome, and conditioning on it biases. Proposal: code act type for the concept from the morphology-free descriptions in 3, not from the word; report L2 with and without act type as distinct estimands.
7. Models: word level, categorical (simple; contrary prefix; *non-*; *-less*), affixes partially pooled within the contrary class, varying intercepts for morphological family and semantic domain, domain-varying rarity slopes (UWA name ability as a domain where positives may be rare); pair level, logistic with domain effects. Judge measurement enters as posterior draws, cut feedback primary. Prior and posterior predictive checks (negation share by valence bin, affix counts, off-diagonal counts).
8. Evidence rules as for H2: 90% interval wholly above a substantively justified threshold = support; wholly below = evidence against an effect that large; else inconclusive. Thresholds fixed and translated into probabilities before prevalence is joined.
