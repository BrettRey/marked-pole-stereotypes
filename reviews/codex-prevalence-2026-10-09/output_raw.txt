# Review Board

Simulate a panel of 3-6 expert reviewers providing structured critique of a paper or chapter. Each reviewer runs as an independent subagent to avoid cross-contamination.

Use this skill for failure analysis and presentation quality. Do not let simulated reviewers decide whether Brett's central idea is worth pursuing: unanimous resistance to a strange, cross-paradigm move can be a novelty signal. Separate "is this argued well?" from "is this worth arguing?"

## Context

- Current directory: !`pwd`
- Project CLAUDE.md: !`cat CLAUDE.md 2>/dev/null | head -30 || echo "No project CLAUDE.md"`

## Setup

### 1. Read the target file

Read the file specified in the argument (or `main.tex` if none given). Read it fully -- the reviewers need the complete text to give useful feedback.

### 2. Find or compose the panel

**First, look for a named board.** Check these locations in order:

1. `advisory-board.md` (project root)
2. `notes/advisory-board.md`
3. `docs/advisory-board.md`

If Brett asks for named researchers, use real scholars whose expertise matches the paper's venue and contribution. Do not substitute generic roles when named researchers are requested.

If a board file exists, use its personas. The file should define reviewer names, expertise areas, and any specific concerns each reviewer should focus on.

**If no board file exists,** compose a panel of 3-6 reviewers based on the paper's topic. For a typical HPC/linguistics paper, a good panel might include:

- **Philosopher of science** (natural kinds, HPC theory, scientific realism)
- **Formal linguist** (generative syntax, category theory)
- **Usage-based/functional linguist** (construction grammar, typology)
- **Methodologist** (experimental design, corpus methods, statistics)
- **Domain specialist** (whatever the paper's empirical focus is)
- **Hostile reviewer** (someone predisposed to reject the framework)

Adjust the panel composition to match the paper. A metaphor paper needs a cognitive linguist. A grammaticality paper needs a philosopher of language. Use judgment.

**Mandatory for any paper using HPC theory:** One reviewer must be the **Projectibility Reviewer**. This reviewer's sole concern is whether the paper treats projectibility as the epistemic payoff of the framework or buries it as a footnote. Specifically:

- Does the paper frame the contribution as "here are mechanisms" (wrong) or "the category is projectible -- it generates predictions -- and here's why" (right)?
- Are mechanisms presented as *explaining* the projectibility, or as *being* the point?
- Does the conclusion cash out in what the category lets you *predict, explain, or inductively generalize*, not just what holds it together?
- Boyd's insight: natural kinds are projectible for a purpose. The slogan is "a profile, stabilised by mechanisms, projectible for a purpose." If the third clause is missing or decorative, this reviewer flags it as a major structural problem.

This reviewer exists because LLMs (including the one generating these reviews) have a deep training-data bias toward the mechanism/clustering side of HPC and systematically underplay projection. The bias is so strong that explicit instructions in CLAUDE.md files are routinely ignored. This reviewer is the structural countermeasure.

**For papers that model human or expert positions as ground truth:** Add a **Target-Heterogeneity Check** to at least one relevant reviewer, usually the methodologist or hostile reviewer. This reviewer asks whether the paper distinguishes stable disagreement from other sources of variance: diachronic change, context-sensitive answering, internal contradiction, terminological drift, survey-item mismatch, ambiguity, and plain confusion. Do not let the review treat all heterogeneity as signal or all smoothing as failure. Ask what kind of heterogeneity the method is supposed to preserve.

**Review-board self-check:** The review board is itself a synthetic expert panel and is vulnerable to the same smoothing problem. Do not treat simulated reviewer agreement as evidence of real field consensus. Watch for over-neat convergence, stereotyped reviewer roles, or reviewers whose criticisms follow too predictably from their labels. Preserve contradictions between reviewers, and in the synthesis flag when the board may be collapsing messy expert heterogeneity into a clean centroid.

**The clean-room lane (use whenever convergence drives a decision).** The self-check above says be suspicious of agreement. This is what to do about it.

If the shared prompt states a frame, a diagnosis, or a hypothesis, then later agreement *about that frame* carries no information: the reviewers correctly perform the analysis they were told to perform, then promote it into a recommendation, and the synthesis counts heads. Model diversity does not fix this. A board spanning Fable, Opus, and Codex still shares one prompt author.

Add two reviewers who receive **the artifact, the goal, and the audience, and nothing else**. No diagnosis, no rationale, no prior findings, no description of what the work was trying to achieve. Ask them what is wrong with it and what they would change.

- If they independently rediscover the main findings, those findings are properties of the artifact. Report them as such.
- If they surface different things, the board was measuring the prompt. Say so in the synthesis and re-weight accordingly.

In the synthesis, separate **convergence on recomputed facts** (specificity, counts, dates, statuses, anything each reviewer derived independently) from **convergence on taste**. Only the first survives a shared prompt.

**The audience-proxy lane (for artifacts with real readers).** Add one or two reviewers told explicitly *not* to critique the artifact, and instead to arrive as a specific reader with a task: a journal editor deciding whether to desk-handle, a search chair assessing a candidate, a researcher deciding whether to cite. Give them a timed procedure (ten-second impression, then sixty-second read), require them to mark each question ANSWERED / PARTLY / NOT ANSWERED, and ask for a friction log of everything they wanted and could not get. These reviewers routinely produce findings no number of domain experts can, because they are answering "what does this cost me with a real reader" rather than "is this well made."

**Do not let the board critique a remedy you proposed yourself.** An analysis and its fix authored in the same sitting share a blind spot. Ask the board to *generate alternatives* instead, then choose among them.

    34	- *Marking submodel (lexical; L1, L2):* formal negation, by affix type, depends on within-valence rarity, total valence and stakes, with terms for morphological process (participial *un-*, adjectival *un-*, *-less* on nouns, opaque Latinate *in-*). L1 and L2 ask whether marking follows rarity at fixed valence (L1 among positive words, L2 among negative words, where stakes also enter).
    35	- *Production submodel (H2, H1):* whether participant *p* produces word *i* for group *g*, against the denominator set, depends on ρ (H2), on marking beyond rarity (H1), on word frequency as accessibility, and on v, with varying intercepts for group, participant and item.
    36	
    37	Where the indicators are weak, the posteriors for these coefficients will be wide, and that is reported along with prior sensitivity. Nothing is dropped as "not run". D1 says in advance how wide they're likely to be. Fitting latent variables across submodels bears on the engine choice (brms or PyMC; still open).
    38	
    39	**What the model can and can't identify (settled by D1's pilot, 2026-10-07).** Any prevalence measure partly lines up with valence, and the data can't tell whether that overlap is real (common traits tend to be desirable) or the measure's own bias (people say desirable traits fit them). So the latent is *within-valence* rarity, and each equation carries a *total* valence term. Identifiable: at the same valence, are rarer traits more often marked (L1, L2) or more often produced as stereotypes (H2)? Is marking associated with production beyond rarity (H1)? **Not identifiable without an extra assumption:** does valence matter once rarity is known? That is the "rarity rather than valence" half of the marked-pole claim, and close to UWA's own argument. The plan therefore claims no rarity-versus-valence contrast. **Settled (Brett, 2026-10-09):** route (b), two prevalence measures whose valence biases differ in a known direction (self-endorsement inflates desirable traits; judged prevalence in others should do so less), where such measures exist; otherwise route (a), one measure with a sensitivity analysis over a stated range of its valence bias. The contrast is claimed only as strongly as the route supports; under (a), as a conditional ("if the measure's valence bias lies between X and Y, valence does or doesn't matter once rarity is known").
    40	
    41	**Which rarity: settled (Brett, 2026-10-09): both perceived and actual prevalence, as far as the data allow.** Approach, in Gelman's spirit (model the measurement rather than choose a construct; where a bias can't be estimated, show how conclusions depend on it):
    42	
    43	1. *Target:* actual prevalence as a latent variable. Effects are also reported for perceived prevalence, which needs fewer assumptions.
    44	2. *Indicators,* each with its own item-level standard errors, its own valence term, and an informative prior on a shared (between-rater) bias: judged prevalence in others (likely bias: availability, i.e. tracking word familiarity or marking, modelled as a dependence on word frequency); self-endorsement norms (likely bias: desirability); behavioural or act-frequency data where they exist.
    45	3. *Identification by what exists.* With two or more indicators whose biases differ, the plausible range for actual prevalence narrows, but actual-prevalence effects stay sensitivity-based: differing biases don't identify actual prevalence without anchors or restrictions on shared bias, and priors supply assumptions, not identification (Codex review, 2026-10-09). With one indicator, perceived prevalence is estimated directly, and actual-prevalence effects appear only as a sensitivity analysis over a stated range of the shared bias.
    46	4. *Simulate first (D1b, before FIXED):* a fake-data check of the multi-indicator design, recovering actual-prevalence effects when the biases differ, and the error of the one-indicator estimate when a shared bias exists.
    47	
    48	Background: D1 converges only when the indicator's noise is known, from item-level standard errors, which capture rater disagreement only; a misjudgment raters share is absorbed into the latent. So a single indicator yields perceived prevalence, and D1's results are a best case. This settlement also serves the availability guard (two indicators with different biases) and reopens the rarity-versus-valence contrast (route (b) above) when the indicators' valence biases differ in a known direction. What can be done depends on the prevalence-norm search (running 2026-10-09).

    50	**Act type (raised 2026-10-09 by the side session's handover; L2 and H1 settled, see "H1 details").** In French (Google Books 1950–2019), whether a bad trait word defers to its antonym in comparatives (*moins honnête que* rather than *plus malhonnête que*) follows act type: failing to do an expected act against doing acts, net of word frequency, logit 1.06 [0.51, 1.56] over 128 concepts. With act type in the model, formal negation has no detectable effect among traits (−0.13 [−0.67, 0.54], 21 prefixed traits), though across all adjectives negated words do defer more (`Project-Management/notes/2026-10-09-marked-pole-brief-for-main-session.md`; numbers checked against `2026-10-08-marked-pole-french-v2/results.md` and `results_1b.md`). Limits: model-coded act type (eight families, no human check), French, comparatives rather than stereotypes. It bears on two things here. (1) H1 measures markedness by formal negation; if the marked pole means the dependent pole, act type may be the better trait-level variable. (2) L2's illustrations confound stakes with act type (*cruel*, *violent* coded 'doing'; *désordonné* 'not doing'). **Settled for L2 (Brett, 2026-10-09): act type is adopted in L2** (see L2 below). Still open, for H1: keep formal negation and say the hypothesis concerns overt coding; add act type (commission against omission) as an observed item-level covariate in the marking and production submodels; or add the comparative measure itself as a functional markedness variable where English counts exist. Act type is close to the "feature absence" sense kept separate in Variables; the French result supports keeping it distinct from formal negation.

    53	
    54	- *What H1 asks.* An exploratory, predictive question: does formal negation improve held-out prediction of word choice beyond within-valence rarity, valence, word frequency and measured word properties? Prediction is evaluated by holding out **words or morphological families**, not participants (participant holdouts leave the word-level problem untouched). The conditional association is also reported as a posterior-predictive comparison averaged over a common-support population of words and groups, with the competing vocabulary fixed and the treatment of frequency (held fixed or integrated over) stated. Predictive improvement remains compatible with confounding, so H1 isn't read as evidence for or against the common-cause account.
    55	- *Why not a test.* D1 scenario 2: an unmeasured register factor produced a marking coefficient of about +.20, with 90% intervals excluding 0 at the large size, where the truth was 0, under both prevalence measures (`results/d1/pilot2_fits.csv`). Only measuring the confounder helps, and adjustment can't rule out unmeasured ones.
    56	- *Causal structures.* Alternative causal diagrams for marking, act type, register, frequency and production are written down before FIXED. Act type may partly reflect meaning created by negation, and register reflects lexical history and use, so neither is assumed to precede marking without argument.
    57	- *Negation and act type.* Separate coefficients for contrary prefixes (*un-*, *in-/im-/il-/ir-*, *dis-*), *non-* and privative *-less*; affixes are pooled only within the contrary-prefix class. A sceptical prior on a pre-specified negation × act-type interaction, with a recovery check under sparse overlap. Act type enters through a calibrated measurement model allowing correlated errors across coder families and genuinely ambiguous items, not as a thresholded code or a raw vote share.
    58	- *Human calibration of act type.* Brett and a second human coder code a stratified random sample of words (strata: affix class, valence, coder-family disagreement, rare act-type categories), blind to the hypotheses and to the model codes, with instructions that guard against "prefixed" becoming shorthand for "omission"; disagreements adjudicated. Sample size is set from the precision wanted for the misclassification rates (roughly 150–300 words), fixed before coding.
    59	- *Register.* Three distinct measures, not one: etymological origin (Latinate against native), present-day register (learnedness or formality), and familiarity (for example Condon, Coughlin & Weston 2022, CC0). Sources still to choose and verify. D1c simulates how much bias survives across control reliabilities, correlations with valence and act type, and differential coding errors.
    60	- *Reporting.* A pre-specified set of specifications (no word-level controls; register; act type; both), which isn't nested, each reported as standardized predictive contrasts on the same target population, with overlap diagnostics and prior sensitivity. No "interpret only if it survives" rule; coefficient movement isn't read as confounding removed. D1 shows bias is possible; it doesn't give false-positive rates.
    61	- *Overlap check.* Unprefixed negative words are informative contrasts too; check the overlap of negation and valence on the frozen word list before relying on the claim that the estimate rests on a small off-diagonal set.

    63	**H1 and the frequency control.** Gelman & Hill (2007, §9.7, p. 188): "it is generally not a good idea to control for variables measured after the treatment." If word frequency is partly a consequence of trait rarity, it's an intermediate outcome, and §9.8 cautions against regressing on intermediate outcomes to estimate mediation. Frequency is modelled jointly as a path, but the production equation still conditions on it, so modelling it jointly doesn't by itself avoid the problem: what an estimate means depends on the predictive averaging, either holding frequency fixed or integrating over its rarity- and marking-dependent distribution, and each estimand must say which (see "H2 details"; corrected 2026-10-09 after the Codex review of H1, `reviews/codex-h1-2026-10-09/`).     74	**L1: rare-positive domains (lexical; no outcome data).** If marking follows rarity, then in an opposition where the positive pole is the rare one, the positive member should carry the negator. Candidate words (assembled from intuition; check against a lexicon before use): *unbeaten*, *undefeated*, *unmatched*, *unrivalled*, *faultless*, *infallible*, *invincible*. Restrict the test to oppositions where exactly one member contains a negator; privative pairs such as *harmless/harmful* and *flawless/flawed* have both members derived. Against the hypothesis: negated positive members are no more common where the positive pole is rarer, measured by prevalence and not by intuition.

If marking follows stakes and not prevalence, the hypothesis's single-rarity claim fails. **Act type (adopted for L2, Brett, 2026-10-09):** each word is coded for act type, naming acts done (commission) or an expected act not done (omission), and act type enters the L2 model as an item-level covariate. 1. **5a — Agree with the association; disagree with the causal justification.** Register and act type are not demonstrably antecedent merely because they describe words. Act type may partly reflect the meaning created by negation; contemporary register may reflect lexical history and use. Specify competing causal structures before choosing adjustment. “Pure common cause predicts zero” requires adequately measured rarity, no additional shared causes, and the appropriate frequency treatment. A positive residual does not establish an effect of marking.

   Define the predictive comparison precisely: average marked–unmarked differences over a common-support distribution of words and groups, keeping the competing vocabulary fixed. Specify whether frequency is held fixed or integrated over its marking-dependent distribution; those estimate different quantities. D1 explicitly includes marking → frequency → production ([README:26–31](scripts/d1/README.md:26)).

2. **5b — Mostly agree, with restraint.** Report formal negation and act type separately. Use a skeptical interaction prior and check recovery under sparse overlap.

3. **5c — Agree with noisy-control simulation; disagree with equating etymology and register.** Latinate origin is a proxy for the proposed mechanism, not its measurement.    Scenario 2 assumes a particular factor independent of valence with fixed loadings ([README:33](scripts/d1/README.md:33)). **P1 is partly defensible; P2 does not identify an availability correction; P3 overstates transportability; P4 needs respondent-level measurement modelling.** The settled sensitivity-based interpretation of actual prevalence is appropriate. Neither convergence nor partial pooling repairs a mismatch between an indicator and its target.

**P1 — Agree on commonness; disagree on treating all endorsement measures as prevalence indicators.**

Ziano commonness is a reasonable perceived-prevalence measure. Self- and average-American ratings should initially remain separate constructs, with their exact prompts and response scales determining their measurement equations. The verified desirability correlations establish associations, **not bias magnitudes or signs relative to actual prevalence** ([search note, opening and “Notes for the plan”](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/notes/prevalence-norms-search-2026-10-09.md:4)).

Relative-to-peers ESCS self-ratings are not, by themselves, prevalence indicators. A mean comparative rating measures perceived standing plus self-enhancement and response behaviour. Even accurate relative standing cannot establish how many people exceed an absolute trait threshold. A universally common trait and a rare trait can have identical distributions of comparative standing.

I would exclude ESCS from the primary prevalence measurement model unless an explicit, defensible bridge connects comparative ratings to prevalence. Otherwise use it as auxiliary endorsement data. Skip act frequencies absent a independently justified mapping. Also define actual prevalence: which population, time period, trait sense and threshold? A latent logit does not supply these definitions ([plan, “Strand B”](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/analysis_plan.md:32)).

**P2 — Agree with sensitivity analysis and simulation; disagree that the proposed availability term is identified.**

Write, schematically, \(C_i=\rho_i+a_C F_i+\epsilon_i\) and \(S_i=\lambda\rho_i+a_S F_i+\eta_i\). Replacing \(\rho_i\) by \(\rho_i+tF_i\), and adjusting both familiarity coefficients, leaves the observations unchanged. Measured familiarity therefore does not identify its shared bias contribution. Informative priors restrict that trade-off; they do not resolve it empirically.

ESCS’s midpoint ambiguity adds another mechanism: unfamiliarity can produce neutral-looking answers rather than a simple additive shift ([search note, ESCS entry](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/notes/prevalence-norms-search-2026-10-09.md:12)). Differing desirability biases need not protect against shared familiarity bias. Nor are the biases “differently signed”: the supplied correlations are all positive.

I would pre-specify sensitivity ranges for shared and indicator-specific familiarity effects, nonlinearities and direct morphological bias. Report where conclusions change. Fixing self-rating familiarity bias to zero would be an explicit identifying assumption, not an availability guard.

**P3 — Agree with overlap counting; disagree with unconditional borrowing.**

The 149 traits are a selected Alicke subset of Anderson, not a representative calibration sample ([search note, “Bottom line”](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/notes/prevalence-norms-search-2026-10-09.md:10)). Transport requires comparable meanings, measurement relationships and covariate support across familiarity, valence, morphology and trait domains.

Count overlaps first, then inspect support and selection without stereotype outcomes. Use held-out calibration traits, deliberately stressing distributional boundaries, and sensitivity to item-class-specific loadings. Successful internal prediction cannot validate transport outside calibration support. Unsupported ESCS-only words should not acquire precise prevalence estimates merely through pooling.

Keep the independently fixed denominator ([plan, H2 details](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/analysis_plan.md:18)), but distinguish target vocabularies across subset analyses. For uncovered words, assess whether marking or stereotype outcomes themselves drive inferred rarity. Compare joint inference with inference restricting that feedback; broad coverage can otherwise disguise circular measurement.

**P4 — Agree on provenance; revise aggregation and scale linking.**

Verify prompts, licences and item identities first: much of the search report remains unverified. Prefer respondent-level ordinal models with rater variation. Simple SEs omit shared-rater covariance and systematic item disagreement; model “unknown word” responses separately where distinguishable.

Shared items permit scale linking only under measurement invariance and a connected overlap structure. Test instrument-specific thresholds and differential item functioning. Linking comparative scales still does not turn them into prevalence measures.

**Before FIXED, D1b must show:**

- Algebraic identification restrictions, including shared familiarity bias.
- Recovery and interval calibration under real coverage, selected calibration items, midpoint ambiguity and non-invariant instruments.
- Null and threshold-sized H2/L1/L2 effects, with misleading precision and sign errors reported.
- Conclusions across pre-specified bias, transport and feedback assumptions, with Monte Carlo uncertainty.
- Compatibility with D1c’s categorical production model ([plan:22](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/analysis_plan.md:22)).

Two corrections are essential: D1b’s promise of recovery “when biases differ” omits necessary restrictions ([plan:46](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/analysis_plan.md:46)); D1’s contraction/convergence criteria describe performance under assumed models, not structural identification ([README:103](/Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/papers/development/marked-pole-stereotypes/scripts/d1/README.md:103)). Known SEs constrain sampling error, not unknown systematic measurement error.
