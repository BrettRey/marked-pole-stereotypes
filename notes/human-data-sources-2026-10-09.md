## Read and verified

- **Ziano, Mok & Feldman (2021)**, *SPPS* 12(6), 1005–1017, OSF https://osf.io/2y6wj/ (CC BY 4.0). "To what extent are these traits common among the average Americans?", 1–7; a common characteristic is "one that the average American would frequently display" (final survey, 2019-05-27 v3). 297 MTurk raters, 40 of 149 traits each; 76–83 ratings per trait. Construct: perceived display frequency for the average American. Details in `analysis_plan.md`, "Commonness judges".
- **Rothbart & Park (1986)**, *JPSP* 50(1), 131–142, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.50.1.131. Scale 7, "Prevalence of trait in the population": "How frequently would you expect to see this trait in the general population?" (Table 1, p. 134). 150 traits: the 84 Katz & Braly (1933) adjectives plus 66 from Anderson (1968) and Norman (1967), chosen across favorability levels including highly unfavorable (p. 133). 11 judges on this scale, α = .84 (Table 2, p. 135); 82 upper-level psychology students in all. Per-trait values printed in standard scores, no per-trait SDs (Appendix, pp. 140–142). Judged frequency correlates .33 with favorability (Table 3, p. 135).
- **Norman (1967)**, *2800 personality trait descriptors: Normative operating characteristics for a university population*, University of Michigan, report 08310-1-T; ERIC ED014738 (https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED014738.pdf, full text authorized). 2,800 terms in 14 lists of 200; each list given to 50 male and 50 female undergraduates (p. 14). Tasks: a synonym or definition, or cross out if unknown; the applicability of each term to self (S) and to three self-selected peers, one liked (A), one the rater was indifferent to (B), one disliked (C), on 0 ("not a good or accurate description"), 1 ("only partly or occasionally descriptive"), 2 ("a particularly good or accurate description") (pp. 14–16); social desirability, 1–9 (p. 15). Appendices 1–14 give N, mean and SD per term and task, for men and women separately (pp. 15–16). Scanned; extraction needed.
- **Hampson, Goldberg & John (1987)**, *European Journal of Personality*, 1(4), 241–258, https://doi.org/10.1002/per.2410010405. 45 British adults rated 573 terms for category breadth ("the diversity of behavioural referents of a trait") and social desirability; breadth values from the 40 most reliable raters; American values where available (Table 1). Breadth, not prevalence.
- **Hofstee (1995)**, *European Journal of Personality*, 9(1), 71–73, https://doi.org/10.1002/per.2410090106. In Hampson et al.'s Table 1, 92 root/denial pairs; in 12 the denial is the more desirable pole (Unafraid, Unaggressive, Conceitless, Non-conforming, Unconventional, Uncritical, Undemanding, Unenvious, Informal, Unprejudiced, Unselfish, Uninhibited); in 10 of the 12 the denial had the larger category breadth, which Hofstee reads as support for a Frequency Hypothesis ("most people, under most circumstances, behave in a socially desirable manner", p. 72). A direct precedent for L1: if breadth tracks frequency, the negated desirable pole is the more frequent one in those pairs.
- **Koch, Bromley, Woitzel & Alves (2024)**, *JPSP* 126(6), 978–997, https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000383, OSF https://osf.io/eadcm/ (public; no standard licence was recorded at the initial check). Prolific perceivers rated their own descriptions of photographed strangers: "please rate how common each descriptor is. Very common descriptions are those that could apply to many people; very uncommon descriptions (unique) are those that apply to only a few people", 7-point. **Permission update, 2026-10-10:** Brett supplied Alex Koch's email reply of 2026-10-09 granting broad permission in response to the request for research use and publication of derived summaries. A follow-up requests citation. Cite the paper and OSF project. This clears the permission blocker; it does not establish a CC BY licence on OSF. Data retrieval and file-level provenance remain to be completed.

## Leads not yet read, or read without usable item-level data

- **Bernhardson & Fisher (1970)**, "Perceptions of social desirability and frequency of occurrence of traits and the probability that the traits will be endorsed", *Perceptual and Motor Skills*, 30(2), 571–576, https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.1970.30.2.571. Read 2026-10-09 (`literature/bernhardson_fisher_1970_perceived_frequency_endorsement.md`): 100 items sampled from Cruse's (1965) 1,647 items; 74 introductory-psychology students estimated "what percentage of their fellow students were characterized by each trait" and 72 others answered true or false; only correlations and overall means are reported (Table 1: mean perceived frequency 43.22%, SD 17.43), with no per-item values, so it gives no item-level data. It notes that "the frequency that the word describing a trait occurs is not important while the frequency that the trait itself is perceived to occur is important" in predicting endorsement.
- **Linehan (1970)**, "Percentage estimates of positive and negative traits within a population as a function of attitude toward the population, trait content, and actual distribution of traits within the population". Metadata only (Elicit); likely a thesis; details unverified.
- Already known and adjacent: Condon, Coughlin & Weston (2022), familiarity for 2,818 adjectives (in the plan as a familiarity source); ESCS self-ratings (Dataverse, CC0); Leistner et al. (2025, German base rates); Wessels et al. (2025, OSF, no licence).

## Exposure log

The main session read Rothbart & Park's whole Appendix (pp. 140–142), so it saw all 150 traits' standard scores on all eight scales, including the frequency score of every negated trait in the list and two root/negation pairs (Dependent/Independent; Truthful/Untruthful). This bears on L1 and on the word-level test, for which Rothbart & Park is a human source; both are reported with and without those 150 words. (Corrected 2026-10-09 after a side agent's note; the first version of this log said only "a few negated traits".) Hofstee's published 10-of-12 result is prior evidence. Ziano et al.'s aggregated means were seen for two unnegated traits only. No stereotype outcome data have been seen.
